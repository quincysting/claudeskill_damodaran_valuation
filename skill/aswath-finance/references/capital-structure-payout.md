## 1. Core frameworks and principles

**The three corporate finance principles.** Every business decision reduces to investing (pick projects earning more than the hurdle rate), financing (find the debt/equity mix that maximizes value), and dividend policy (return what you can't reinvest well). The financing principle sits at the nexus of the other two — financing choices affect both what you can invest in and what you can pay out ([Deja Vu In Turkey](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/08/deja-vu-in-turkey-currency-crisis-and.html), 2018-08).

**What counts as debt.** Three criteria: it creates contractual/fixed payments due in good times and bad, it's tax deductible, and failure to pay causes loss of control. All interest-bearing debt qualifies. Leases qualify (contractual, tax-deductible, loss of the asset/site on default) — for retailers and restaurants, leases can be 80–90% of "debt." Accounts payable/supplier credit generally does *not* qualify unless you can break out the implicit interest (discount forgone) from cost of goods sold. Underfunded pension/healthcare obligations should *not* be added to debt for cost-of-capital purposes (no legal payment obligation in most countries), though they should be netted out later when moving from firm value to equity value ([What is debt?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/what-is-debt.html), 2009-03; reaffirmed in [Jan 2019 Data Update 7](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-7-debt-neither.html), 2019-02).

**The debt/equity trade-off.** Real benefits of debt: tax deductibility of interest, and a discipline effect on managers of mature, cash-rich firms. Real costs: higher probability and cost of distress/bankruptcy, plus agency costs (covenants, monitoring) from the stockholder–bondholder conflict. "Illusory" benefits that get cited but shouldn't be: debt is cheaper than equity (true in isolation, but ignores that using more debt raises the cost of equity proportionately, leaving the *weighted* cost of capital as the only number that matters), and EPS "accretion"/avoiding dilution ([Capital Structure: Optimal or Opportunistic?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/capital-structure-optimal-or.html), 2010-09; [Jan 2018 Data Update 8](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/01/january-2018-data-update-8-debt-and.html), 2018-01; [Jan 2019 Data Update 7](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-7-debt-neither.html), 2019-02).

**Two ways to find "optimal" capital structure.** (1) Cost of capital: the debt/equity mix minimizing WACC maximizes firm value if operating cash flows are held fixed. (2) Adjusted Present Value (APV): value the firm unlevered, then add the marginal value of debt (PV of tax benefits minus expected bankruptcy costs); the dollar debt level that maximizes this net value is optimal ([Capital Structure: Optimal or Opportunistic?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/capital-structure-optimal-or.html), 2010-09).

**Opportunistic vs. optimal capital structure.** Firms don't actually solve for a target ratio; behavioral-finance evidence (citing NYU colleague Jeff Wurgler's work) shows they issue debt when they perceive debt as "cheap" and issue equity when they perceive their stock as overpriced — "opportunistic capital structure." Danger: CFOs are poor market timers (like the rest of us), and short-term opportunism can leave a firm over-levered once rates/spreads mean-revert. Damodaran's compromise: set a *range* for the optimal debt ratio (e.g., 20–40%, not a point estimate of 30%) so opportunism is bounded ([Capital Structure: Optimal or Opportunistic?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/capital-structure-optimal-or.html), 2010-09).

**Debt design should mirror asset characteristics.** The "perfect" debt gives you the tax benefits of debt while behaving like equity — cash flows that flex with operating cash flows. Practically: match maturity (long-term assets funded with long-term debt), currency (debt in the currency your cash flows are in), and structure (convertible debt for growth companies, commodity-linked rates for commodity businesses). Mismatches should be hedged with derivatives/swaps if you can't match directly ([Deja Vu In Turkey](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/08/deja-vu-in-turkey-currency-crisis-and.html), 2018-08).

**Illiquidity changes all three principles.** If capital markets are illiquid: (a) Investment — firms can't take every positive-NPV project, so a percentage-return metric (IRR) may allocate scarce capital better than raw NPV; discount rates rise with illiquidity. (b) Financing — the optimal debt ratio moves toward whichever market (debt or equity) is *more* liquid, since illiquidity raises both the probability and the cost of distress. (c) Dividend policy — illiquid-market firms should hold more cash and prefer dividends (visible, certain cash) over buybacks (which depend on being able to sell shares back into a liquid market) ([Corporate finance in illiquid markets](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/12/corporate-finance-in-illiquid-markets.html), 2010-12).

**Hybrid securities: decompose, don't treat as a third category.** Convertible debt = a straight-debt component (value at the pre-tax cost of debt for straight debt of similar maturity) + an equity/option component (market value of convertible minus the straight-debt value). Treating the convertible's low coupon as "the cost" understates true financing cost and biases capital-structure decisions ([Dealing with Hybrids](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/dealing-with-hybrids-convertibles.html), 2009-03).

**Preferred stock is "neither fish nor fowl."** U.S.-style fixed-dividend preferred behaves like very expensive, non-tax-deductible debt (fixed claim, no upside), so treat it as debt-like for cost of capital, using Preferred dividend yield = Preferred dividend per share / Preferred stock price as its cost (higher than pre-tax cost of debt, lower than cost of equity). Latin-American-style "common stock" preferred (dividends as % of earnings, price appreciation) should be treated as equity. If preferred is under 5% of firm value, ignore it for cost-of-capital purposes — not worth the complexity ([Preferred Stock: Fish or Fowl?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/preferred-stock-neither-fish-nor-fowl.html), 2009-03; [Is preferred stock equity?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/10/is-preferred-stock-equity.html), 2008-10).

**Why preferred stock gets used despite its cost.** Financial firms use it because regulators count it as regulatory equity for capital ratios (a "bad law creates bad financing choices" case). Young/distressed firms use it because debt's tax advantage is worthless when you're not paying taxes anyway, and investors accept the risk for cumulative dividends plus a conversion option ([Who uses preferred stock?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/who-uses-preferred-stock.html), 2009-03).

**Financial-service firms are structurally different.** Debt (including deposits) is raw material, not a source of capital, to a bank — so enterprise value and cost-of-capital constructs are close to meaningless for banks/insurers. You can only value bank *equity*, using equity multiples (P/E, P/B), not EV multiples ([A tangled web of values](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/06/a-tangled-web-of-values-enterprise.html), 2013-06; [Good (Bad) Banks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/good-bad-banks-and-good-bad-investments.html), 2023-05).

**Banking business model and what separates good from bad banks.** A bank borrows (deposits + debt) at one rate and lends/invests at a higher rate; profitability is the interest-income-minus-interest-expense spread, net of credit/investment losses. Good banks have: (1) sticky deposits, ideally with a high share of non-interest-bearing deposits; (2) larger equity/Tier-1 buffers, preferably built from real common equity rather than equity-like hybrids; (3) loans priced with a fair (or better) spread over default risk, diversified across borrowers; (4) investment securities matched in duration to the deposit base and appropriately marked to market. Deposit stickiness is driven by depositor size (small = stickier), homogeneity, age of relationship, and (in 2023) social-media-driven contagion speed ([Breach of Trust: Decoding the Banking Crisis](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/breach-of-trust-decoding-banking-crisis.html), 2023-05).

**Cash return (dividend) policy: three narratives on buybacks.** (1) *Benign*: cash return is the residual step after good investments are funded; buybacks vs. dividends is just a mechanical choice. (2) *Short-termist*: managers with equity-linked pay favor buybacks (which don't create an ex-dividend price drop) over dividends, chasing EPS/price pops the market doesn't fully risk-adjust for. (3) *Malignant*: buybacks become the organizing purpose of the firm, crowding out wages, capex, and prudent leverage. Damodaran argues the data supports (1) for the majority of firms, with (2) present at the margin and (3) rare ([Jan 2019 Data Update 8](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-8-dividends.html), 2019-02).

**Buyback value effects — a four-way framework.** Whether a buyback changes intrinsic value depends only on (a) how it's funded and (b) whether it changes what the firm invests in operations: *Indifferent* (funded from surplus cash, doesn't affect investment) — no value effect. *Good*: (i) eliminates a "cash discount" the market was applying because it distrusted management with idle cash; (ii) an under-levered firm raises debt to buy back stock and tax benefits exceed added bankruptcy cost, lowering WACC; (iii) redirects cash away from bad (sub-hurdle-rate) investments. *Bad*: (i) an already correctly- or over-levered firm borrows more, raising WACC; (ii) cash that would have funded good (above-hurdle-rate) investments is diverted instead ([Stock Buybacks: They are big...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/09/stock-buybacks-they-are-big-they-are.html), 2014-09).

**Buyback pricing effects (separate from value effects).** Even a value-neutral buyback can move the stock price via: (1) *mispricing/wealth transfer* — if under(over)priced, non-tendering shareholders gain (lose) at tendering shareholders' expense, with no aggregate value creation, only a transfer; (2) *signaling* — markets read buybacks as management's under-valuation signal, though the evidence that managers are skilled market-timers is weak; (3) *liquidity* — large buybacks relative to daily trading volume can temporarily move price ([Buybacks and Stock Prices: Good or bad news?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/buybacks-and-stock-prices.html), 2011-01; [Stock Buybacks: They are big...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/09/stock-buybacks-they-are-big-they-are.html), 2014-09).

**Four structural differences between dividends and buybacks.** (1) Dividends are sticky (expected to persist or grow); buybacks are not, and can be turned on/off without penalty. (2) Buybacks shrink share count and thus ownership structure; dividends don't. (3) Dividends pay all shareholders pro rata; buybacks pay only those who tender, creating possible wealth transfers. (4) Tax treatment differs by jurisdiction/era — buyback participants control the *timing* of their capital-gains tax, dividend recipients don't ([Stock Buybacks: They are big...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/09/stock-buybacks-they-are-big-they-are.html), 2014-09).

**Rethinking dividend policy for volatile-earnings companies.** Because dividends are sticky by tradition (Lintner, 1956) and cutting them is treated as a last resort, firms facing more globalization/earnings volatility have systematically shifted cash return toward buybacks or reset toward variable payout mechanisms: fixed payout ratio (pay X% of net income — problem: net income isn't cash flow), residual FCFE payout (pay a fixed % of Free Cash Flow to Equity = Net Income + Depreciation − CapEx − ΔWC, adjusted for net debt), or contingent dividends tied to a macro driver (e.g., oil price for an energy company) ([Dividend Policy for the 21st century](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/dividend-policy-for-21st-century.html), 2011-02).

**Default is not binary.** Default is conventionally "failure to make a contractual payment," but Damodaran argues it should be defined more broadly as *any* situation where lenders accept a reduction in the value of their claim to avoid triggering technical default — loosened covenants, restructured terms, below-market refinancing. Governments/banks often prefer this "implicit default" over explicit default because it delays recognition of losses (avoiding capital write-downs and reputational damage), but this just defers and often enlarges the eventual reckoning (cites Japanese banks in the 1990s as the cautionary case) ([Default and Bankruptcy: Black, white and shades of grey](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/07/default-and-bankruptcy-black-white-and.html), 2011-07).

**The implicit guarantee problem.** A large share of global lending rests on implicit (non-contractual) guarantees — sovereign backstops for "too big to fail" institutions, parent-company backstops for subsidiaries, family-group backstops for related firms. Because none of the three parties (lender, borrower, guarantor) makes the obligation explicit, everyone can misjudge true leverage; firms that look lightly levered on a standalone balance sheet can be effectively highly levered once the implicit guarantee is priced in ([Dubai and the "implicit" guarantee](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/12/dubai-and-implicit-guarantee.html), 2009-12).

**Predicting the effects of financial reform: three counterintuitive calls on Dodd-Frank (2010).** Reviewing the ~2,000-page US financial-overhaul bill (Volcker Rule, exchange-traded/cleared standard derivatives, a new Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 5% securitization risk retention, rating-agency litigation exposure) against its stated goal of preventing another banking crisis, Damodaran made three predictions that reframed the debate beyond "tougher regulation = safer banks": (1) the bill would *not* reduce "too big to fail," because nothing in it caps bank size, and the compliance burden itself would advantage the largest banks (economies of scale in regulatory navigation), pushing them to get *bigger* and more opaque, not smaller and more transparent; (2) it would *not* durably reduce "bad" risk-taking, because the incentive driver is asymmetric compensation (upside captured by decision-makers, downside socialized), not the specific instruments (swaps desks, prop trading) being restricted — banks would find new, unforeseen risk channels, so "the next crisis will look nothing like the last one"; (3) counterintuitively, banks would likely become *less profitable* (lower ROE, pushed back toward safer/lower-return businesses) but *more valuable*, because the same regulatory compliance burden that erodes ROE also raises barriers to entry, protecting incumbent banks' excess returns from new competition ([Will the financial overhaul bill fix what's wrong with banks?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/06/will-financial-overhaul-bill-fix-whats.html), 2010-06).

**Multi-business breakups only create value if fundamentals change.** A break-up creates *value* only if it changes cash flows, growth, cost of capital, or growth duration versus the consolidated entity — not automatically. Multi-business firms often mis-set a single hurdle rate, a single capital structure, and a single dividend policy across businesses with different risk/growth/asset profiles, which is where a break-up can genuinely help (capital reallocation, tailored leverage, tailored payout). Separately, a break-up can raise *price* (not value) purely by letting the market apply more appropriate peer multiples to each piece ([Focus Pocus](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/11/focus-pocus-is-breaking-up-cure-for.html), 2014-11).

**Systemic risk is an asymmetric-payoff problem, not a "too much risk" problem — so the fix is symmetry, not a new regulator.** Reacting skeptically to the 2009 proposal for a new "Agency for Financial Stability" (which would have stripped bank oversight from the Fed): the real defect in the pre-2008 system was that risk-takers captured the upside of systemic bets while taxpayers/the system absorbed the downside — a payoff asymmetry, not a supervisory-agency-design problem. Damodaran's preferred remedies target the asymmetry directly rather than adding a bureaucratic layer: (1) monitor compensation structures so they don't reward systemic (house-money, macro) bets more than firm-specific ones; (2) no bailouts — firms that make bad systemic bets should fail, with assets clawed back to cover losses; (3) a pre-funded systemic-risk fund, financed by a levy on profits from systemic risk-taking itself, walled off from legislative raiding ([The Agency for Financial Stability? Good idea?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/11/agency-for-financial-stability-good.html), 2009-11).

**What a Credit Default Swap actually is, why the market grew the way it did, and what its price tells you.** A CDS is default insurance on a specific bond issuer (government or corporate): the buyer pays an annual premium (quoted in basis points of notional) and receives the notional if the reference entity defaults. Devised by JPMorgan bankers in the late 1990s as a hedging tool, the market exploded through the 2000s to a notional value exceeding $50 trillion by 2008 — bigger than the actual bond market, meaning much of the "insurance" being bought and sold referenced securities that didn't even exist net of it, i.e., most CDS activity was speculative, not hedging. Two reasons to buy: hedge an existing default-risk exposure, or bet the reference entity's default spread will widen (buy low, resell high). Two reasons to sell: earn transaction-volume income as a matched-book intermediary, or bet the spread will narrow — with the standing danger (AIG, Lehman) that a seller collects premium income now and defers/ignores the tail liability. Because both counterparties (not just the reference entity) can default, CDS carry embedded counterparty risk, which is why even a CDS on a default-free entity trades at a non-zero price, and why CDS spreads jump market-wide (not just on the referenced names) whenever counterparty-risk perceptions rise, as they did after Lehman's failure ([The Credit Default Swap (CDS) Market](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/02/credit-default-swap-cds-market.html), 2010-02).

**Rogue trading: why it recurs, how traders lose hundreds of millions, and how they evade detection.** Rogue trading is trading that violates an employer's risk limits and exposes the firm to catastrophic risk — critically, it is *not* defined by losses: a rogue trader who wins big is celebrated and often goes on to start a hedge fund; only the losers are prosecuted, which is itself part of why the incentive to take the bet persists. Three behavioral drivers recur across cases (Kerviel/SocGen, Kweku Adoboli/UBS, Nick Leeson/Barings): (1) *selection bias* — trading floors are disproportionately staffed by young, single men, the least risk-averse demographic subgroup by experimental-economics evidence; (2) the *house-money effect* — traders are careless with the firm's capital in a way they would not be with their own; (3) the *break-even effect* — a trader nursing a loss takes progressively bigger, less rational bets trying to claw back to zero, the same dynamic that keeps gamblers at the table. Three ways large losses get built without early detection: leveraging via borrowed capital, via derivatives with theoretically unlimited downside (futures, short options), or via long-short strategies (large gross exposure funded by a small net capital base); and three reasons the losses stay hidden: mark-to-market/risk-reporting time lags (worse for ETFs and other basket instruments), systematic model error that traders learn to exploit (e.g., a VaR model assuming a normal distribution understates the tail risk of assets prone to price jumps), and simple denial by the people positioned to notice early ([Jerome Kerviel is sentenced](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/10/jerome-kerviel-is-sentenced-ruminations.html), 2010-10; [Ruminations on Rogue Trading](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/09/ruminations-on-rogue-trading.html), 2011-09).

## 2. Methods and procedures

**Bank equity valuation — pre- and post-2008.** Pre-2008 default approach: dividend discount model, assuming (i) bank managers pay out what they can sensibly afford and (ii) regulators keep risk-taking in check. Post-2008 approach (the current method): a bank-specific FCFE model, because conventional CapEx/working-capital concepts don't exist for banks:
`Bank FCFE = Net Income − Increase in regulatory capital needed for growth`
This "regulatory capital reinvestment" plays the role CapEx plays for a normal firm, and FCFE can go *negative* (unlike dividends, which floor at zero) if a bank needs fresh equity to survive. The cost of equity should reflect duration mismatch, capital-ratio buffer, and loan/investment book risk. For a stable-growth bank: Payout ratio = 1 − (Expected growth / Return on equity) ([Breach of Trust: Bank Valuation](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/03/breach-of-trust-bank-valuation-after.html), 2011-03; [Good (Bad) Banks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/good-bad-banks-and-good-bad-investments.html), 2023-05).

**Bank price-to-book decomposition.** From a stable-growth DDM, dividing through by book equity: P/BV is driven by (ROE − g)/(Cost of equity − g), i.e., a bank should trade above (below, at) book value if expected ROE exceeds (is below, equals) its cost of equity. Damodaran builds a "cheapness scorecard" across ROE, Tier-1 ratio, deposit growth (lower = stickier = better), and % of securities held-to-maturity, coloring each green/red versus the peer median, then requires low P/B *plus* a preponderance of green fundamentals to call a bank genuinely underpriced (used Citigroup as the illustrative "cheap for the right reasons" case in May 2023) ([Good (Bad) Banks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/good-bad-banks-and-good-bad-investments.html), 2023-05).

**Capitalizing operating leases as debt (Damodaran's method since the 1990s, pre-dating the 2019 accounting rule change).** (1) Estimate the firm's current pre-tax cost of debt. (2) Discount each year's disclosed lease commitment back at that rate (convert any lump-sum "after year 5" figure into an annuity first, rather than dumping it all in year 6). (3) Sum of PVs = lease debt, added to the balance sheet with an offsetting leased asset. (4) Recompute operating income: `Adjusted Operating Income = Stated Operating Income + Current-year lease expense − Depreciation on the leased asset` ([Jan 2019 Data Update 7](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-7-debt-neither.html), 2019-02).

**Two families of debt-load metrics, and why they disagree.** Value-based: Debt/Capital using book value vs. market value (book ratios are far less reliable outside financials since accounting book value has "lost relevance" for most sectors). Earnings/cash-flow-based: Debt/EBITDA (overall load) and Interest Coverage = EBIT/Interest expense (immediate safety buffer, more tied to cost of debt and default risk). Country rankings can flip completely depending which metric you pick — e.g., in the 2018 data, China ranked highest on gross Debt/EBITDA but average on net debt; India looked lightly levered on Debt/EBITDA but exposed on interest coverage ([Jan 2019 Data Update 7](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-7-debt-neither.html), 2019-02).

**The net-debt effect on P/E ratios.** Cash is a PE "upper," debt is a PE "downer" (both operate through risk, not just through the numerator/denominator mechanics). In a worked numerical example (10% cost of capital, 40% tax rate), the PE is 11.81 with no cash and no debt but 12.66 with 40% debt and 40% cash — the cash effect dominates the debt effect. Adding cash raises the blended PE because the market implicitly prices riskless cash income at a much higher multiple (a function of the riskfree rate and tax rate) than risky operating income; adding debt does the reverse: it raises EPS but also the riskiness of equity returns, pushing PE down even though value per share can still rise (up to the point where the marginal cost of debt exceeds the breakeven rate, ~8.6% in the example). A separately useful decomposition across the market: `Debt effect ≈ EV/EBIT(1-t) − Non-cash PE` ([Cash, Debt and PE Ratios](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/06/debt-cash-and-pe-why-cash-is-upper-and.html), 2015-06). Practical rules: don't compare P/E across companies or across time without controlling for cash and debt differences; any transaction that changes net debt as a % of value (buyback, especially a debt-funded one) should mechanically change the "fair" P/E, so applying today's P/E to post-buyback EPS overstates value.

**Enterprise-value measurement pitfalls (the "tangled web").** *Equity side*: watch for non-traded share classes with different rights, unvalued management options, and unconverted convertible securities. *Debt side*: non-traded (mostly bank) debt is usually booked at book value — fine for healthy firms, badly wrong for distressed ones where market value << book value; off-balance-sheet debt (leases) must be added back. *Cash side*: separate operating cash needs (no clean rule — some use 2% of revenue as a rough cut) from excess cash; watch for "trapped" cash (foreign earnings that trigger tax on repatriation — flagged specifically for Apple's >$100B trapped cash in 2013) which the market may discount below face value. *Cross-holdings*: minority stakes should be marked to market where possible, not left at book; majority stakes require adding back minority interest (itself only a book-value proxy) since consolidated debt/cash already reflect 100% of the subsidiary. *Consistency*: numerator/denominator of any multiple must match — equity value with equity earnings/book value (P/E, P/B), enterprise value with operating income/book capital (EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT) ([A tangled web of values](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/06/a-tangled-web-of-values-enterprise.html), 2013-06).

**Optimal-capital-structure "cost of capital" mechanics.** Build a schedule: at each hypothetical debt ratio, estimate the (rising) pre-tax cost of debt given implied rating/default spread, the (rising) cost of equity via a levered beta, and the after-tax WACC; the debt ratio that minimizes WACC is optimal and coincides with maximum firm value if operating cash flows are unaffected. Damodaran re-ran this for Disney/Facebook/Ford after the 2017 U.S. tax reform to show that lower marginal tax rates + an interest-deduction cap at 30% of adjusted taxable income (EBITDA-based until 2021) mechanically pushes optimal debt ratios and the value added by debt down at every leverage level ([Jan 2018 Data Update 8](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/01/january-2018-data-update-8-debt-and.html), 2018-01).

**Institutional defenses against rogue trading, and who else should price in the risk.** Beyond hiring for demographic diversity on trading desks, four systems-level fixes: real-time (not batch/lagged) risk measurement; restricting trading in illiquid, appraisal-priced assets that risk models handle poorly; simpler, narrower risk systems that are harder to game than complex ones stuffed with easily-exploited statistical assumptions (borrowing the El Al airport-security model of profiling the highest-risk activity rather than monitoring everything equally); and stress testing on the premise that every risk system eventually fails, so the response to failure matters more than the (temporary) appearance of success. Two further prescriptions beyond internal risk management: (1) *bank managers* allocating capital across businesses should require proprietary trading to clear a higher hurdle rate than commercial/investment banking, since it embeds this catastrophic tail risk; (2) *bank stock investors* should treat the proportion of a bank's profits sourced from proprietary trading as a risk-quality screen — between two banks priced at the same earnings multiple, prefer the one with less trading-driven revenue; (3) *regulators* should bar deposit-backstopped commercial banks from proprietary trading altogether (an explicit endorsement of the Volcker Rule) ([Ruminations on Rogue Trading](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/09/ruminations-on-rogue-trading.html), 2011-09).

**Valuing a claim on an intermediated/layered cash-flow stream (e.g., tracking stock on a person's future earnings) — a five-layer risk taxonomy generalizable to any structured or securitized claim.** When investors don't have a direct claim on the underlying cash-generating asset but instead hold a claim on what an intermediary chooses to pass through, value the chain in stages: (1) *underlying-asset earnings risk* — for a person, career-ending injury/performance decline risk (model as an annually compounding survival probability) and contract-default risk; (2) *macro/structural risk* on that income (collective-bargaining caps, macroeconomic sensitivity of any market-linked component); (3) *intermediation risk* — the manager (Fantex, in the source case) may spend a large, uncapped share of collected income on activities (brand-building) with no guaranteed payoff, and may pool your claim with other assets in its portfolio, exposing you to losses on unrelated positions ("spillover risk"); (4) *governance risk* — as a non-voting claim holder you cannot compel the intermediary to distribute residual cash, unlike an equityholder's claim on retained cash/book value; (5) *investment/illiquidity risk* — a novel, thinly-traded claim needs an explicit illiquidity premium in the discount rate. Worked example (Fantex/Arian Foster, Oct 2013): a $10M payment for 20% of career income was valued, ex-injury-risk, at ~$10.06M to Fantex (roughly a wash), but the *tracking stock* investor's claim — after management fees (5%), brand-building spend (15%), a +3% discount-rate premium for zero governance power, and a +3% illiquidity premium — was worth only ~$6.11/share against Fantex's proposed ~$10 offering price; incorporating career-ending injury probability (cumulative to 37% by year 9) pushed the claim value down further, to ~$5.07/share ([Valuing Athlete Earning Potential? Tracking Arian Foster!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/10/game-on-tracking-stock-on-arian-foster.html), 2013-10).

**Fixing (or scrapping) fairness opinions in M&A — a judicial screening questionnaire and four structural reforms.** A fairness opinion is a paid outsider's attestation that a deal's price is fair "from a financial point of view"; the practice isn't legally mandated but became near-universal after *Smith v. Van Gorkom* (Delaware, 1985) created a safe harbor for boards that obtained one. In current practice (Damodaran's direct extension of the Tesla/SolarCity "Keystone Kop Valuations" critique — see Myths), fairness opinions have degraded into a liability shield for boards rather than genuine investor protection, with a Gresham's-law dynamic where managers shop for the most accommodating appraiser, driving rigorous appraisers out of the business. Damodaran's proposed judicial screening questionnaire, with green/red flags a judge (who need not be a valuation expert) can apply: is any of the appraiser's fee contingent on the deal closing (FINRA Rule 2290 requires disclosure)? Are cash flows independently estimated or simply supplied by management? Are currency, claim (equity vs. firm), and growth/reinvestment assumptions internally consistent? Is the discount rate within the normal range for the sector? Is terminal growth below the economy's growth rate, with matching reinvestment/risk? Are arbitrary control/synergy premiums or illiquidity/size discounts being layered on top? Does the output present a base case with a real distribution, or a range so wide any price is "possible"? Four structural reforms beyond the questionnaire: (1) deal-making bankers should never also opine on their own or peers' deal fairness (a banker who blesses today's deal will want reciprocal treatment on tomorrow's); (2) ban deal-contingent fees for fairness-opinion work entirely; (3) force appraisers to pick a lane — genuine intrinsic valuation (own the cash-flow assumptions, use defensible discount rates, no ad hoc premiums) or genuine relative pricing (justify the specific multiple and comparable set) — not an unaccountable hybrid; (4) require a probability *distribution* of value, not a range, since "is this price possible?" is nearly always yes while "is this price plausible?" is the real, harder question (demonstrated with Damodaran's own May 2016 Apple Monte Carlo valuation, where a $160 price fell at the 92nd percentile — possible, but neither plausible nor probable). The post also proposes real consequences for repeat offenders — forced return of fees, removal from the transaction, and personal (not shareholder-funded) sanctions for boards/managers who rely on opinions that fail the test ([Fairness Opinions: Fix them or Flush them!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/09/fairness-opinions-fix-them-or-get-rid.html), 2016-09).

**The "augmented dividend" concept, and three different investor playbooks for the dividend-to-buyback shift.** As buybacks overtook dividends (60% vs. 40% of S&P 500 cash return by 2010), Damodaran proposed tracking Augmented Dividends = Dividends + Stock Buybacks (and derived Augmented Dividend Yield/Payout from it) as the metric investors should actually use instead of dividend-only figures — the same "net cash yield" logic later formalized in the 2019 data-update methodology (see above), but this 2011 post is its origin and adds investor-type-specific guidance: (1) the *dividend player* (classic high-yield strategies like "Dow Dogs") should treat buybacks as noise and keep screening on dividends, but recognize that the remaining big dividend-payers are, if anything, sending an even *stronger* confidence signal now that buybacks offer companies an easy alternative they're choosing not to take; watch for "impostors" — unstable-earnings firms sustaining an oversized dividend to retain a loyal yield-seeking shareholder base; (2) the *cash player*, indifferent between dividends and buybacks as a source of near-term cash, should use augmented-dividend metrics but recognize buybacks are far more volatile than dividends (aggregate US dividends fell ~10% in 2009 while buybacks fell ~80%) and should favor under-levered firms with few investment opportunities as buyback candidates, avoiding over-levered firms buying back stock with borrowed money; (3) the *growth player* should read a buyback announcement at a young/growth company as a possible signal that hyper-growth is ending (reassess the thesis), but can view buybacks at a mature company as a way to convert it into a de facto growth investment via price appreciation as other holders tender ([The Shift to Buybacks: Implications for investors](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/shift-to-buybacks-implications-for.html), 2011-02).

**Futures-spot arbitrage mechanics, and why a single large trade can move an entire index (the 2010 "Flash Crash").** The no-arbitrage condition pins the futures price to the spot price: Futures Price = Spot Price × (1 + risk-free rate − dividend yield). If a large sell order distorts the futures price away from this relationship, arbitrageurs' trades (borrow, buy the spot index, sell futures short — or the reverse) force the spot price to follow the futures price, not the other way around, transmitting a shock from a purely liquidity-driven trade in one market into the "cash" price of the entire index. In the May 6, 2010 Flash Crash, one trader at a single Kansas-based asset manager (Waddell & Reed) sold ~75,000 e-mini S&P 500 futures contracts (9% of volume in that window) during an already-jittery session (Greek-default fears had the market down 250 points); other traders, reading the size and anonymity of the order as a signal of superior information, followed the selling, and the futures-spot linkage transmitted the shock into a roughly 1,000-point intraday Dow swing before both markets reversed within the hour. Damodaran's policy verdict: this was a liquidity-driven aberration, not a case for banning or heavily restricting futures trading — long-term, intrinsic-value investors bear little cost from occasional flash-crash volatility (and can treat the panic as a buying opportunity), while the cost falls mainly on short-term traders, "who are short-term traders by choice" ([\"We are not in Kansas anymore\"](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/05/we-are-not-in-kansas-anymore.html), 2010-05).

**Cash-return measurement should net stock issuance and fix R&D accounting.** Gross cash yield = (Dividends+Buybacks)/Market cap overstates true cash return because it ignores new equity raised; Net cash yield nets out issuances: (Dividends+Buybacks−Issuances)/Market cap. Similarly, cash "payout ratios" that look alarmingly high (S&P 500 cash returned exceeded net income in 2015 and 2016) are partly an artifact of expensing R&D (a long-duration investment) instead of capitalizing it; capitalizing R&D and adding it back to earnings materially lowers the computed payout ratio ([Jan 2019 Data Update 8](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-8-dividends.html), 2019-02).

## 3. Rules of thumb and heuristics

- Preferred stock under 5% of firm value: ignore it for cost-of-capital purposes; above 5%, give it its own cost-of-capital slot ([Preferred Stock](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/preferred-stock-neither-fish-nor-fowl.html), 2009-03).
- Terminal-value growth rate should never exceed the risk-free rate in perpetuity — flagged Evercore's 6–8% "forever" growth assumption for Tesla as indefensible ([Keystone Kop Valuations](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/09/keystone-kop-valuations-lazard-evercore.html), 2016-09).
- High-dividend-yield screens: avoid payout ratios above 80% combined with negative revenue growth (signals a liquidating, not sustainable, dividend); favor companies with positive earnings, low debt, high cash; diversify across sectors rather than mechanically taking the highest-yield names market-wide (which cluster in one or two sectors, e.g., banks pre-2008) ([High dividend stocks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/10/high-dividend-stocks-do-they-beat.html), 2010-10).
- Set a *range*, not a point estimate, for a target debt ratio, so opportunistic financing decisions stay bounded ([Capital Structure: Optimal or Opportunistic?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/capital-structure-optimal-or.html), 2010-09).
- To check whether a buyback is skewing leverage market-wide, compare debt ratios of buyback vs. non-buyback companies in the same universe — in the 2013 U.S. data they were not meaningfully different ([Stock Buybacks: They are big...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/09/stock-buybacks-they-are-big-they-are.html), 2014-09).
- Watch quarterly (not just year-end) balance sheets and book-vs-effective interest rates to catch "window dressing" of debt (borrow, pay down before fiscal year-end, re-borrow after) ([Stock versus Flow](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/04/stock-versus-flow-my-thoughts.html), 2010-04).
- For an "underpriced bank" screen, look for a *combination* of low P/B, above-median ROE, above-median Tier-1 ratio, and low deposit growth (stickier deposits) — no single metric suffices ([Good (Bad) Banks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/good-bad-banks-and-good-bad-investments.html), 2023-05).

## 4. Myths and mistakes Damodaran calls out

- **"Buybacks always push the stock price up."** False in general; whether price moves and in which direction depends on how the market was pricing the firm's cash (discount vs. premium), the firm's leverage before/after, and whether operating-asset value itself changed. The "dilution delusion" — assuming a constant P/E applied to post-buyback EPS — ignores that the P/E itself should fall as leverage or risk rises ([Buybacks and Stock Prices](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/buybacks-and-stock-prices.html), 2011-01; [Stock Buybacks: They are big...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/09/stock-buybacks-they-are-big-they-are.html), 2014-09).
- **"Debt is cheaper than equity, so more debt lowers the cost of capital."** True only of the *component* cost, not the weighted average; using more debt raises the cost of equity roughly proportionately ([Jan 2018 Data Update 8](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/01/january-2018-data-update-8-debt-and.html), 2018-01).
- **"Buybacks are corporate cocaine / are destroying the U.S. industrial base" (Economist/Schumer–Sanders narrative).** The 2018 U.S. data show buyback companies were *not* disproportionately debt-funding their buybacks (similar or lower Debt/EBITDA than non-buyback firms), capex at buyback firms was similar to or higher than non-buyback firms, and the biggest buyback sectors (tech, pharma) are low-debt, R&D-heavy — the "under-investment" story is largely an artifact of expensing R&D. Restricting buybacks would likely just redirect cash into bad reinvestment or overseas expansion, not U.S. factories or wages ([Stock Buybacks: They are big...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/09/stock-buybacks-they-are-big-they-are.html), 2014-09; [Jan 2019 Data Update 8](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-8-dividends.html), 2019-02).
- **"Convertible debt's coupon is its cost."** No — the low coupon reflects the embedded option; using it as "the cost of convertible debt" makes convertibles look artificially cheap and biases capital-structure choice ([Dealing with Hybrids](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/dealing-with-hybrids-convertibles.html), 2009-03).
- **"A negative enterprise value is free money."** Usually an artifact of (a) missing off-balance-sheet debt like leases, (b) stale year-end cash balances vs. current market cap, or (c) sloppy conflation of market value of equity with market value of the firm — not a real arbitrage ([Enterprise value is negative](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/12/entperise-value-is-negative-is-that.html), 2008-12).
- **"Borrowing in a lower-nominal-rate foreign currency is cheaper."** Ignores that the lower rate typically reflects lower expected inflation in that currency, not a real cost saving — a classic (and recurring) emerging-market CFO error, seen again in Turkey in 2018 ([Deja Vu In Turkey](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/08/deja-vu-in-turkey-currency-crisis-and.html), 2018-08).
- **"CFOs can time whether debt or equity is cheap and should act on it."** The evidence on market-timing skill (portfolio managers, and by extension CFOs) is poor; opportunistic capital structure decisions risk over-leveraging once conditions revert ([Capital Structure: Optimal or Opportunistic?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/capital-structure-optimal-or.html), 2010-09).
- **"Outsourcing your DCF's cash flows to someone else and just plugging in a discount rate is 'valuing' the company."** Evercore and Lazard, in the Tesla/SolarCity deal, used cash flows supplied respectively by Goldman Sachs equity research and by SolarCity management — "if the CF in a DCF is supplied to you by someone else, you are not valuing the company" ([Keystone Kop Valuations](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/09/keystone-kop-valuations-lazard-evercore.html), 2016-09).
- **"'Focus' as a break-up rationale is self-evidently value-creating."** It's often strategy-buzzword cover; a break-up only creates value if it demonstrably changes cash flows, growth, risk or cost of capital versus staying combined — most CEO break-up rationales don't clear that bar ([Focus Pocus](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/11/focus-pocus-is-breaking-up-cure-for.html), 2014-11).
- **"CDS speculators/short sellers are causing sovereign defaults" and "a single flash-crash trade is proof markets/derivatives need to be banned or heavily restricted."** Both blame the messenger for the message: rising CDS spreads on Greece/Portugal reflected a decade of prior fiscal mismanagement, not the other way around; the May 2010 Flash Crash was a liquidity-driven aberration transmitted mechanically through the futures-spot arbitrage relationship, not evidence that futures markets are unsound — and the investors who actually bear a cost from such events are short-term traders, by their own choice of strategy ([The Credit Default Swap (CDS) Market](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/02/credit-default-swap-cds-market.html), 2010-02; ["We are not in Kansas anymore"](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/05/we-are-not-in-kansas-anymore.html), 2010-05).
- **"S&P downgrading the U.S. from AAA is a catastrophe."** The information content of ratings changes is usually already priced in beforehand (ratings agencies "follow markets, rather than lead them"); the real danger is entities fighting the ratings agency instead of adjusting policy ([Chill, dude!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/08/chill-dude-it-is-not-ratings-downgrade.html), 2011-08).

## 5. How Damodaran's views evolved

- **Bank valuation methodology, pre- vs. post-2008.** Pre-crisis: dividend discount model, trusting bank management to pay a sensible dividend and trusting regulators to police risk-taking. After 2008 exposed both trust assumptions as false, Damodaran shifted to a bank-specific FCFE model tying cash flow to changes in regulatory capital, explicitly allowing for negative FCFE (need to raise equity) and pricing failure risk separately from the going-concern DCF ([Breach of Trust: Bank Valuation](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/03/breach-of-trust-bank-valuation-after.html), 2011-03; reaffirmed and refined in [Good (Bad) Banks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/good-bad-banks-and-good-bad-investments.html), 2023-05, after SVB).
- **Post-2008 predictions about capital structure that only partly played out.** In January 2009 Damodaran predicted debt ratios would fall as the trade-off tilted against debt (higher distress probability/cost) and companies would shift toward flexible cash return (buybacks) ([Corporate Finance - post crisis](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/01/corporate-finance-post-crisis.html), 2009-01). The buyback shift did happen dramatically (see §6), but aggregate U.S. non-financial debt did *not* meaningfully fall even after the 2017 tax reform cut the benefit of debt by ~40% — Damodaran attributes this to inertia, uncertainty about permanence of the tax cut, and lingering belief in "illusory" debt benefits ([Jan 2019 Data Update 7](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-7-debt-neither.html), 2019-02).
- **From "risk-free U.S. Treasury" to "nothing is truly risk-free."** Damodaran used the U.S. T-bond unquestioningly as the riskfree rate for three decades, but dates the loss of faith to September 2008 (money-market funds "breaking the buck," negative T-bill yields), crystallized further by the 2011 debt-ceiling standoff and S&P downgrade — after which Damodaran began explicitly backing a default spread out of the T-bond rate to get a "true" USD riskfree rate ([Chill, dude (Part II)](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/10/chill-dude-part-ii-debt-default-drama.html), 2013-10).
- **Dividend policy prescriptions loosening over time.** From describing dividends as inherently "sticky" (Lintner-style) as simply a fact of corporate life in 2008–2009, Damodaran moved by 2011 to actively advocating alternative policies (fixed payout ratio, residual FCFE payout, contingent dividends) as better matches for equity's residual-claim nature, given rising earnings volatility from globalization ([Sticky dividends!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/12/sticky-dividends.html), 2008-12 → [Dividend Policy for the 21st century](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/dividend-policy-for-21st-century.html), 2011-02).
- **Debt definition converges on lease capitalization becoming mainstream.** Damodaran had been manually capitalizing leases as debt for decades before IFRS/GAAP finally required it starting fiscal 2019; the rule change is treated as vindication rather than new information, arguing markets had already priced leases as debt long before accountants caught up ([Jan 2019 Data Update 7](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/02/january-2019-data-update-7-debt-neither.html), 2019-02).

## 7. Cases and examples

- **Proposed "Agency for Financial Stability" (2009).** Senator Dodd's proposal to strip banking oversight from the Fed and create a dedicated systemic-risk regulator — Damodaran's rebuttal argued the Fed's competence/independence wasn't the real problem (no regulator anywhere foresaw the 2008 crisis) and that the actual fix needed was addressing payoff asymmetry (compensation, no-bailout commitments, a systemic-risk fund), not a new bureaucratic layer; used as a template for why "let's create an agency" is often a category error in response to a genuine problem ([The Agency for Financial Stability?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/11/agency-for-financial-stability-good.html), 2009-11).
- **Greek/Portuguese sovereign CDS blame game (2010).** As Greek and Portuguese default risk rose, both governments publicly blamed CDS market speculation for deepening their funding troubles — Damodaran's rebuttal: CDS spreads are a messenger reporting default-risk information (which is itself a function of a decade of prior fiscal choices), not a cause of that risk, and "it makes no sense to shoot the messenger" ([The Credit Default Swap (CDS) Market](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/02/credit-default-swap-cds-market.html), 2010-02).
- **The May 6, 2010 "Flash Crash."** A single Waddell & Reed trader's e-mini futures sell program, transmitted into the spot market via futures-spot arbitrage, produced a ~1,000-point intraday Dow swing that fully reversed within about an hour — a clean natural experiment in how derivatives-market liquidity shocks propagate into headline equity indices without any change in fundamentals ([\"We are not in Kansas anymore\"](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/05/we-are-not-in-kansas-anymore.html), 2010-05).
- **Dodd-Frank financial overhaul bill (2010).** ~2,000-page reform package assessed against its own stated goal of preventing the next crisis — Damodaran's three predictions (bigger/more opaque big banks, a still-unaddressed compensation-driven risk-taking problem, and banks trading lower ROE for higher franchise value/excess returns) reframed "tougher regulation" as neither a clean win nor a clean loss for investors ([Will the financial overhaul bill fix what's wrong with banks?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/06/will-financial-overhaul-bill-fix-whats.html), 2010-06).
- **Jerome Kerviel/Société Générale (2008, sentenced 2010) and Kweku Adoboli/UBS (2011).** Two rogue-trading episodes (€5B and $2B in losses, respectively) used as paired case studies for the selection-bias/house-money/break-even behavioral framework and the leverage/detection-evasion mechanics; Adoboli's UBS "Delta One" desk (nominally a low-risk derivatives/ETF arbitrage business) illustrates how ostensibly placid trading functions can still provide the tools (derivatives, ETFs) for outsized, hidden risk-taking ([Jerome Kerviel is sentenced](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/10/jerome-kerviel-is-sentenced-ruminations.html), 2010-10; [Ruminations on Rogue Trading](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/09/ruminations-on-rogue-trading.html), 2011-09).
- **Fantex/Arian Foster tracking stock (Oct 2013).** First-of-its-kind security tracking a share of an NFL running back's future contract and endorsement income; Damodaran's layered valuation (career-ending-injury probability, age-related performance decline, intermediation/brand-building risk, zero governance power, illiquidity) priced the public tracking share at ~$5–6, well below Fantex's proposed ~$10 offering reference price — illustrating that novel securitized/intermediated claims need the same layered-risk decomposition as any structured product, not a simple pass-through assumption ([Valuing Athlete Earning Potential?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/10/game-on-tracking-stock-on-arian-foster.html), 2013-10).
- **Michael Dell/Silver Lake management buyout of Dell (2013) and the Tesla/SolarCity fairness opinions (2016).** Delaware Vice Chancellor Travis Lester's ruling that Dell should have been valued at $17.62 rather than the $13.75 buyout price — one of the rare instances of a court rejecting a fairness opinion — is flagged as a case chosen on shaky reasoning (short-term market underpricing after heavy investment) even though the underlying instinct to challenge fairness opinions was right; contrasted with Lazard/Evercore's Tesla/SolarCity opinions (cash flows supplied by Goldman Sachs research and by SolarCity management itself, a 6–8% perpetual growth assumption, a values range wide enough to justify almost any price) as the template for what a fairness-opinion crackdown should actually target ([Fairness Opinions: Fix them or Flush them!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/09/fairness-opinions-fix-them-or-get-rid.html), 2016-09).
- **US/UK bank preferred-stock bailouts (Oct 2008).** Both governments called preferred-stock injections "equity," but Damodaran flagged the fixed non-deductible dividend as debt-like. The UK terms (higher dividend, dividend blackout until preferred repaid, greater government control) were more punitive to common stockholders than the U.S. terms, and UK bank stocks (Lloyds, HBOS, RBS) fell on the announcement while U.S. bank stocks rose slightly — evidence the market read the same instrument very differently depending on structure ([Is preferred stock equity?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/10/is-preferred-stock-equity.html), 2008-10).
- **New York Yankees infield contracts as "debt."** Discounting the Yankees' guaranteed player contracts (Teixeira, Cano, Jeter, A-Rod, Posada, Sabathia) at a 6% pre-tax cost of debt yields ~$561M of "debt," pushing the effective enterprise value of the team well above the $1.5-2B Forbes equity estimate — illustrating that fixed contractual commitments (like airline lease-heavy fleets) are economically debt no matter what the balance sheet says ([The Yankee infield and debt](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/yankee-infield-and-debt.html), 2009-03).
- **Simmons Bedding LBO (Thomas H. Lee Partners).** Used to argue that LBO analysis shouldn't fixate on leverage as villain; a genuine LBO candidate needs all three value levers (financial leverage/tax arbitrage, control/governance improvement, public-to-private flexibility) — Simmons and the earlier failed Harman Audio LBO (Goldman/KKR, where Harman had limited excess debt capacity, was already well-run, and had a large CEO-stockholder) were poor LBO candidates ([Leveraged Buyouts](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/10/leveraged-buyouts.html), 2009-10).
- **Dubai World default (Nov–Dec 2009).** A government-linked entity's near-default rattled markets disproportionately because it exposed the fragility of "implicit guarantee" lending (UAE oil wealth assumed to backstop a city-state's overreach) — a template Damodaran extends to too-big-to-fail institutions and Asian/Latin family-group companies with debt-laden unconsolidated subsidiaries ([Dubai and the "implicit" guarantee](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/12/dubai-and-implicit-guarantee.html), 2009-12).
- **Procter & Gamble, 2001–2010 cash return.** P&G paid out 41.65% of net income as dividends, but 110% of net income and 120% of FCFE once buybacks were included — meaning the firm net-borrowed to fund total cash return, illustrating the volatility (spike in 2006) that a fixed-dividend-plus-variable-buyback policy creates versus a smoother fixed-payout or residual-FCFE alternative ([Dividend Policy for the 21st century](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/dividend-policy-for-21st-century.html), 2011-02).
- **Buffett/Bank of America preferred deal (Aug 2011).** Buffett's $5B preferred (6% cumulative, redeemable at 5% premium) plus warrants for 700M shares at $7.14 — Damodaran values the warrants at ~$3B, making the effective preferred investment ~$2B earning $300M/year, a ~15% effective yield, while BofA got no Tier-1 capital benefit, no tax deduction, and lost dividend/buyback flexibility; concludes the deal was lucrative for Buffett and value-destructive optics for BofA shareholders regardless of which "true state of the bank" story you believe ([Buffett and Bank of America](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/08/buffett-and-bank-of-america-poker-and.html), 2011-08).
- **Apple/David Einhorn preferred-stock proposal (2013).** Einhorn proposed Apple issue perpetual preferred to existing shareholders to unlock value from its ~$137B cash pile; Damodaran's rebuttal (the "hundred-dollar-bills" analogy) is that for the plan to work, the preferred must trade at a premium to fair value at scale, which Damodaran doubts survives issuance sizes anywhere near Apple's cash hoard, and that the case requires empirical evidence from the actual preferred market, not assumption ([Hundred dollar bills are hard to come by!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/02/hundred-dollar-bills-are-hard-to-come-by.html), 2013-02).
- **Tesla/SolarCity merger valuations by Evercore and Lazard (2016).** Flagship example of "banking malpractice": inconsistent growth/reinvestment linkage, static discount rates through a maturing forecast period, mixing DCF with pricing multiples for terminal value, cash flows outsourced to Goldman Sachs equity research (Tesla) and to SolarCity management itself (SolarCity), and a perpetual growth assumption of 6–8% (Evercore/Tesla) — plus a separate $400M debt double-counting error by Lazard ([Keystone Kop Valuations](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/09/keystone-kop-valuations-lazard-evercore.html), 2016-09).
- **eBay/PayPal break-up (2014).** Value-creation channels tested and mostly rejected (corporate cost cuts unlikely; competitive-advantage gains unclear; a debt-ratio increase to 20-30% added only $479-655M) except a plausible growth-unleashing story for PayPal (20% vs 15.49% growth adds ~$13.6B); separately, a pricing-based analysis (applying online-retail multiples to the marketplace unit and payment-processor multiples to PayPal, margin-adjusted) reproduced roughly the pre-breakup enterprise value (~$69B), suggesting the deal was more about correcting investor mispricing (wrong comparables bundled together) than about creating fundamental value ([Focus Pocus](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/11/focus-pocus-is-breaking-up-cure-for.html), 2014-11).
- **2018 Turkish Lira crisis.** Turkish non-financial firms' FX-debt/FX-asset mismatch (currency and maturity) — much of it sourced from Turkish, not foreign, banks — turned a currency collapse into a corporate solvency crisis; recurring pattern across 1994, 2001, 2008, and 2018 Turkish crises, attributed to both "dangerous" (currency speculation, "everyone does it" moral hazard) and outright "bad" (misunderstanding that a lower nominal foreign rate can still be the more expensive real rate) reasons for mismatching debt ([Deja Vu In Turkey](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/08/deja-vu-in-turkey-currency-crisis-and.html), 2018-08).
- **Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, First Republic (2023).** SVB: deposit base doubled in 2021 (unusually unsticky, concentrated in VC/tech), 55–60% of assets in long-duration Treasuries/MBS funded by short-duration deposits (duration mismatch), overstated Tier-1 capital pending mark-to-market recognition. Signature: >90% uninsured deposits, flagged by FDIC for "poor management." First Republic: wealth-management client base with large, mobile, >$250K deposits — Q1 2023 deposit outflow of 41% forced realized losses on security sales. Across the 641 publicly traded US banks examined, the 2017–2022 deposit-growth rate (a stickiness proxy) explained more of the 2023 market-cap decline than size or loan-book composition ([Breach of Trust](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/breach-of-trust-decoding-banking-crisis.html), 2023-05).
- **Citigroup vs. JPMorgan Chase, May 2023.** JPM: superior ROE, growth, and Tier-1 ratio, but priced at a much higher P/B — a "good bank, full price" case. Citi: subpar ROE (8.11% in 2022) and low growth, but trading near 0.5x book with strong Tier-1 ratio and low deposit growth (stickier deposits) — Damodaran's bank-FCFE valuation (assuming 3% perpetual risk-adjusted asset growth, ROE recovering to its 5-year average of 9.50% against an 11.67% implied cost of equity for large banks, Tier-1 target of 15%) valued Citi at ~$69 vs. the $46.32 market price — a "bad bank, cheap enough to be a good investment" case added to Damodaran's own portfolio ([Good (Bad) Banks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/05/good-bad-banks-and-good-bad-investments.html), 2023-05).
- **COVID crash, buybacks/dividends and market damage (March 2020).** Companies that bought back stock in 2019 lost slightly more market cap (~25% globally, ~26% in the US) between Feb 14 and Mar 27, 2020 than non-buyback firms (~21%/~23%), and dividend-payers suffered similarly — but the effect was concentrated in negative-EBITDA buyback firms (-31% vs -24% for non-buyback peers) and in the *lowest*-debt buyback quintile (-26% vs -16%), undercutting a simple "buybacks caused the crash damage" narrative ([A Viral Market Meltdown V](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/03/a-viral-market-meltdown-v-bailouts-and.html), 2020-03).
