## 1. Core frameworks and principles

- **An investment philosophy is not a strategy or a slogan — it is a belief system about *where and why markets make mistakes and how they correct*, from which strategies follow.** "Buy low, sell high" is a slogan; screening for low-PE stocks is a strategy; the belief that markets underprice boring, low-growth businesses is the philosophy underneath it ([Finding your investing lodestar](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/03/finding-your-investing-lodestar-in.html), 2026-03). There is no single best philosophy — only the one that fits a given investor's beliefs and psychological make-up ([The secret to investment success: Self Awareness?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/11/secret-to-investment-success-self.html), 2010-11; [Value Investing I](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/10/value-investing-i-back-story.html), 2020-10).

- **The master organizing map: three binary choices define where a philosophy sits in the investment process** ([Finding your investing lodestar](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/03/finding-your-investing-lodestar-in.html), 2026-03):
  1. *Active investing vs. passive indexing* — is beating the average worth the attempt?
  2. *Investing vs. trading* — do you act on value (cash flows, growth, risk) or on price (mood, momentum, demand/supply)? Price traders use charts/volume; information traders trade around news releases; arbitrageurs exploit cross-market mispricing.
  3. *Market timing vs. stock/asset picking* — do you bet on whole markets/asset classes being mispriced, or on individual securities within a market taken as given? Market timing pays more if right, but is much harder to do well than stock picking.
  Philosophies can be melded (e.g., "contrarian value investing") only if they (a) share a core belief about the *type* of market mistake and (b) have one designated as dominant to break ties.

- **Value vs. growth investing is not about caring about value — both camps do. It is about *where* in the "financial balance sheet" (assets-in-place vs. growth assets) each camp believes market mispricing is easiest to find and exploit** ([Value Investing: An Identity Crisis?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/06/value-investing-identity-crisis.html), 2012-06; [Value Investing I](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/10/value-investing-i-back-story.html), 2020-10; [A Viral Market Update VIII](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/05/a-viral-market-update-viii-crisis-test.html), 2020-05). Value investors bet on mature companies with mispriced existing assets; growth investors bet on mispriced future growth. Four ways to define/practice value investing: "lazy" (low PE/PBV), "cerebral" (Graham/Buffett qualitative overlay), "big data" (statistically mined extra factors), and Damodaran's own four-way split into passive, contrarian, activist, and minimalist (tilted-index) value investing.

- **Markets are "efficient enough" for most people, most of the time — not perfectly efficient.** Damodaran's working definition: a market is efficient "if you define an efficient market as one where investors cannot easily take advantage of market imperfections... markets are efficient to most investors on most assets most of the time" ([Buffett and Munger... Shock value!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/05/buffett-and-munger-shock-value.html), 2009-05). Damodaran is not a strict EMH adherent but believes 99% of investors would do better acting as if markets were efficient, given transaction costs.

- **Behavioral finance reshapes who profits and how fast, not the mechanics of intrinsic value itself.** Expected cash flows are still expected cash flows and required returns still must reflect risk; what behavioral economics adds is insight into (a) why analysts' value estimates diverge, (b) why price diverges from value, and (c) how quickly price converges on value ([Behavioral Economics: Thoughts on Value and Price](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/03/behavioral-economics-thoughts-on-value.html), 2011-03). Damodaran was a behavioral-finance skeptic until the weight of evidence changed that view (explicit self-described conversion, 2009–2011).

- **Price and value are different processes with different drivers and can diverge for long periods.** Value comes from fundamentals (cash flow, growth, risk) via intrinsic or accounting-based estimation; price comes from market demand/supply, driven by mood, momentum and incremental information ([A Violent Earnings Season](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/02/a-violent-earnings-season-pricing-and.html), 2016-02; [Value Investing III](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/10/value-investing-iii-requiem-rebirth-or.html), 2020-10). Investing is a bet that price converges to value; trading is a bet on price movement irrespective of value.

- **Diversification is a function of (a) how confident you are in your value estimate and (b) how confident you are that price will converge to that value within your horizon — not a fixed rule.** More uncertainty about value → more diversification, not less; more capacity to *cause* convergence (activism, control) → less diversification needed; longer time horizon → less diversification needed ([How much diversification is too much?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/11/how-much-diversification-is-too-much.html), 2011-11; reaffirmed in [The Situational Awareness Blow-up](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/08/the-situational-awareness-blow-up.html), 2026-08). You can pick stocks and be diversified at the same time — the false choice is concentration vs. index-hugging, not concentration vs. diversification ([Value Investing III](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/10/value-investing-iii-requiem-rebirth-or.html), 2020-10).

- **Investment conviction is a continuum, built from three separable beliefs: (1) your fair-value estimate is more right than the market's, (2) the market will correct toward it, and (3) that correction happens within your holding period** ([The Situational Awareness Blow-up](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/08/the-situational-awareness-blow-up.html), 2026-08). Conviction should scale position size and leverage — but leverage magnifies both correct and incorrect convictions, and can truncate your time horizon by forcing liquidation before a correct thesis plays out ("truncation risk").

- **Mean reversion is the (often unexamined) load-bearing assumption behind most of value investing and market timing.** Both "time series" mean reversion (a metric like CAPE reverts to its own historical average) and "cross-sectional" mean reversion (a stock's multiple reverts to peer averages) assume the underlying distribution is *stationary* — an assumption broken by structural change (company aging, technology/industry disruption, shifting investor/consumer preferences) ([Mean Reversion: Gravitational Super Force or Dangerous Delusion?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/08/mean-reversion-gravitational-super.html), 2016-08).

- **Successful investing rests on three qualities that have nothing to do with modeling technique, drawn from an extended analogy to Brunelleschi's Duomo in Florence** (a dome designed and built 1420-1436 by studying, then reverse-engineering, construction methods from the thousand-year-older Pantheon in Rome, whose own building techniques had been lost). *Faith*: the patience to hold a value estimate through an extended period where price hasn't converged to it, without losing confidence in either your own judgment or the market's eventual correction — the post contrasts Damodaran's own impatience (growing uncertain within months) with a culture willing to fund a cathedral for decades before anyone could engineer its dome. *Humility*: "there are no new investment lessons, just old ones to relearn" — better data and models create an illusion of sophistication, but investors keep repeating mistakes their predecessors already made and declared "never again." *Art and science together*: the false choice between "story tellers" and "number crunchers" is really one discipline, not two competing ones — Brunelleschi needed both engineering and aesthetic judgment to build a structure with no precedent, just as valuation needs both narrative and numbers ([Investing and Valuation Lessons from the Renaissance](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/08/investing-and-valuation-lessons-from.html), 2016-08).

- **"Impactful" information is not the same as "insider" information, and US insider-trading law has quietly shifted from regulating* who* trades to regulating *what* is traded on.** The original 1934 Act targeted defined corporate insiders; case law (Rajaratnam/Gupta, Martoma) has broadened enforcement to whoever trades on "material, non-public" information, regardless of employment status. Damodaran classifies all price-*impactful* information into three families — **fundamental** (company/sector/macro, each split into company/government-generated vs. independently-collected-by-outsiders), **trading/technical** (volume, order flow, sentiment/mood data), and **processed** (analyst estimates, newsletters, sector research) — and argues good insider-trading policy has to sort this grid into "clearly insider" (company news releases, government macro data — should be regulated), a "gray area" (exchange order-flow/trading data, paid premium news feeds), and "not insider, leave to markets" (analyst revisions/recommendations, newsletters, survey data, independently-produced macro indicators), rather than either banning all impactful-information trading or (the Friedman/Manne extreme) banning none of it ([Impactful information is not always insider](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/03/impactful-information-is-not-always.html), 2014-03).

- **Five guiding principles for judging any insider-trading rule or market-structure proposal** ([Impactful information is not always insider](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/03/impactful-information-is-not-always.html), 2014-03): (1) company disclosure should be unbiased, complete, and timely; (2) there should exist a strong *external* information market (independent analysts/investors) to supplement and challenge corporate releases; (3) the market's reaction to information should be both appropriate in magnitude and immediate; (4) price should reflect all available information, public or private, as quickly as possible; (5) investors must perceive the process as fair, or they exit the market entirely. Banning all trading on impactful information satisfies (5) but kills the incentive behind (2) and pushes information into a black market; allowing all of it satisfies (2) and (4) but undercuts (1) and (5) — there is no free lunch, only a trade-off to manage.

- **Information timing/frequency itself is a policy lever with the same trade-off structure, whether the discloser is a company (quarterly vs. semi-annual reporting) or a central bank (routine forward guidance vs. silence).** Advocates of *less* frequent/less-guided disclosure argue it reduces short-term gaming and refocuses attention on fundamentals; defenders of the status quo argue that withdrawing scheduled information doesn't eliminate the game, it just pushes it into whatever information source remains (and creates more room for insiders to profit at public investors' expense in the gap) ([Information Timing and Release: The Gaming of Guidance!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html), 2026-07). Damodaran's own resolution, straddling both sides: keep quarterly reporting (it updates valuations and its earnings-day price corrections act as catalysts for over/undervalued positions), but gut its *content* — eliminate lawyer-driven risk-factor boilerplate and forward-looking guidance sections (which Damodaran separately shows *caused* much of the growth in report length once companies began giving guidance in the 1990s), keeping only what happened, not managed predictions of what will happen. The post explicitly declines to invoke "short-termism" as a justification either way, calling the term incoherent in practice (people label unfavorable price moves "short-term" and favorable ones "market wisdom") and noting short-term traders supply liquidity that long-term investors also depend on.

## 2. Methods and procedures

- **The "earnings game" mechanics, and four ways to try to play it** ([Earnings surprises, price reaction and value](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/07/earnings-surprises-price-reaction-and.html), 2012-07; updated [Information Timing and Release](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html), 2026-07): an earnings "surprise" is defined relative to expectations (consensus analyst estimate, or historical growth trend), not the raw magnitude of the earnings change — a 30% earnings increase can be bad news if 40% was expected. Three robust empirical patterns around announcements: (i) a mild pre-announcement price drift consistent with the eventual surprise direction; (ii) an announcement-day price move (historically ~3% for the most positive decile); (iii) a post-announcement drift that continues for about 30 days in the same direction as the surprise (post-earnings-announcement drift). Cross-sectional differences: value (low-PE/PBV) stocks historically got more positive announcement-day reactions than growth stocks (suggesting markets are systematically over-optimistic about growth-company expectations); smaller firms show bigger reaction and drift; management commentary, not just the EPS number, moves prices; and reactions are larger at high-institutional-ownership firms. Four ways investors try to exploit this: (a) predict the surprise before it happens (edges into insider-information risk); (b) trade the post-announcement drift directly (small edge, ~2–3%, better suited to leveraged/options plays); (c) screen for "earnings momentum" (a multi-quarter history of beats) as a factor; (d) do the intrinsic-value version — look past the EPS headline to the components (revenue growth, margin, ROIC) and flag cases where a "beat" masks value-negative underlying trends, or vice versa, as a contrarian trading opportunity.

- **Firms actively manage both sides of the earnings-surprise game, and by 2026 the payoff to doing so had weakened.** Companies "work" analyst expectations down via selective (if now Reg-FD-constrained) signaling and "work" the actual number up via accounting choices and discretionary timing (e.g., R&D spend timing) — producing a persistent excess of positive over negative surprises. As the game became more transparent (analyst estimates and revisions now freely visible on Zacks/Yahoo/Google), the market devised a countermeasure — **"whispered earnings,"** an unofficial, history-based bar above the published consensus that a serial beater must clear to register as a genuine positive surprise (Apple: beat whispered estimates in 42 of 56 quarters as of mid-2012). By 2026, this arms race had eroded the very relationship it fed on: the link between earnings surprises and price reaction had become "weak, with a significant subset of positive surprises resulting in price drops" — a direct reversal from the cleaner decile-by-decile pattern documented in 2012, attributed to public information about estimates and revisions being priced in earlier and more completely ([Earnings surprises, price reaction and value](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/07/earnings-surprises-price-reaction-and.html), 2012-07; [Information Timing and Release](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html), 2026-07).

- **Disclosure/report bloat has four identifiable drivers, and one of them is guidance itself.** A typical quarterly earnings report ran 2,000–5,000 words in 1980 and had tripled or quadrupled in size by the turn of the century, with word counts still rising since, driven by: (1) accumulating accounting-rule-mandated disclosures (some merited — e.g., stock comp — some reactive/knee-jerk after scandals); (2) macro-crisis-driven spikes (visible jumps in 2008–09 and 2020); (3) lawyer-written risk-factor sections providing legal cover, which Damodaran reports never having found "useful in a valuation"; (4) the rise of management **guidance** itself — a practice that barely existed before the 1990s, accelerated after the 1995 Private Securities Litigation Reform Act ("Safe Harbor" law) and Reg FD (2000, which banned selective analyst leaks and pushed firms toward formal guidance instead), peaked above 50% of companies giving earnings guidance around 2003, and has since receded to roughly a fifth of firms — even as reports overall kept growing ([Information Timing and Release](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html), 2026-07; connects to the earlier ["disclosure diarrhea"](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2021/07/disclosure-dilemma-when-more-data-leads.html) argument).

- **Reporting frequency itself is not a one-way historical ratchet — it has reversed repeatedly outside the US.** The US moved annual (1934) → semi-annual (1955) → quarterly within 45 days (1970), with the NYSE privately mandating quarterly reports for most listed firms as early as 1939 and over 60% of public companies voluntarily reporting quarterly even before any mandate (1931). Elsewhere the trend has round-tripped: the UK and EU both imposed mandatory quarterly reporting in 2007 and rolled it back to semi-annual by 2013–14; Singapore narrowed its 2003 quarterly mandate in 2020; Japan adopted quarterly reporting in 2003 and reversed it in 2024. India and Nigeria require quarterly reporting, as is true for many Brazilian and Chinese companies; South Africa and much of the rest of Africa use semi-annual. Lesson: there is no international consensus that more-frequent disclosure is unambiguously better, and the optimal cadence is a live, reversible policy choice, not a settled fact ([Information Timing and Release](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html), 2026-07).

- **Five-step passive-value screening blueprint** ([Passive Value investing: Screening for bargains](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/06/passive-value-investing-screening-for.html), 2012-06):
  1. Screen for price — pick a multiple, keep numerator/denominator consistent (equity multiple ↔ equity denominator; enterprise-value multiple ↔ firm-wide denominator); use EV multiples when leverage varies widely across peers.
  2. Screen for risk — operating, financial, liquidity risk, via price-based, accounting-based, sector-proxy, or sector-specific screens.
  3. Screen for growth — consistent with the multiple chosen, forward-looking where possible.
  4. Screen for quality of growth — ROE/ROIC vs. cost of equity/capital, or sector-specific efficiency measures.
  5. Rinse and repeat — loosen screens if the sample is too small; add/adjust screens if the result is sector-concentrated.
  Two implementation modes: "bludgeon screening" (screens alone build the portfolio) vs. "screening plus" (screens narrow the universe, then intrinsic valuation or qualitative judgment picks winners).

- **Damodaran's own personal-portfolio discipline against denial/panic/hubris** ([The Search for Investment Serenity](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/04/the-search-for-investment-serenity-look.html), 2015-04):
  1. *Due process*: revalue every position in the portfolio at pre-specified intervals (at least annually, plus around major news), not just the ones you feel like checking.
  2. *Spread bets*: stay diversified across many names, because concentration makes both panic and denial worse for any single position.
  3. *Be explicit*: keep valuation assumptions written down and shared publicly, so errors and self-serving tweaks get caught.
  4. *Admit mistakes publicly*: forces the "clean slate" needed to revalue honestly.
  5. *Faith without doctrine*: hold conviction that price converges to value strongly enough to survive short-term adverse moves, but be willing to revise the thesis if facts consistently contradict it.

- **Activist value investing's three value-creation levers**, applied to a target once identified as poorly run ([Activist Value Investing](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/06/activist-value-investing-be-your-own.html), 2012-06): (1) generate more cash from existing assets (efficiency/cost cutting), (2) invest for more valuable growth (reinvest more where returns exceed cost of capital, less where they don't — this is a dividend-policy lever too), (3) lower the cost of funding (better capital structure/debt matching). Damodaran frames expected value as `Optimal Value × P(management change) + Status Quo Value × (1 − P(management change))`.

- **Constrained-contrarian screening recipe for avoiding value traps** — start with steep losers, then layer quality/safety filters ([Buy the Dip](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/04/buy-dip-draw-and-dangers-of-contrarian.html), 2025-04): Damodaran's illustrative April 2025 screen was (a) market cap > $100m, (b) lost >20% of market cap in a 3-week window, (c) PE < 15 (bottom quintile), (d) dividend yield > 1% (i.e., a dividend payer, in a year 60% of US stocks paid none), (e) net debt/EBITDA < 2 (bottom quintile leverage) — only 6 of ~6,000 US stocks passed all filters.

- **Opportunistic contrarianism as a two-stage process**: (1) build a watchlist of businesses you consider "great" *before* any crisis, even while they're overvalued — do not force the valuation to make them look cheap; (2) during a market-wide selloff, revalue those same companies from scratch with crisis-adjusted cash flows and risk premiums, and buy only if the *new* value exceeds the *new* price — never compare a post-crash price to a pre-crash valuation ([Buy the Dip](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/04/buy-dip-draw-and-dangers-of-contrarian.html), 2025-04).

- **Testing a market-timing rule properly requires four explicit specifications, not just a correlation**: (1) a timing threshold (how extreme must the metric be to trigger action), (2) the destination asset class for reallocated money, (3) a holding period, (4) allocation constraints (floor/cap, long-only vs. short-allowed) ([Mean Reversion](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/08/mean-reversion-gravitational-super.html), 2016-08). "Correlation is not causation" matters less to an investor than "correlation is not cash in the bank" — a statistically significant regression (e.g., CAPE-implied 10-yr return) still has to survive transaction costs, taxes, and out-of-sample structural change to become a strategy.

## 3. Rules of thumb and heuristics

- Ben Graham's original ~10 screens (E/P > 2× AAA bond yield, PE < 40% of 5-yr high, price < ⅔ tangible book, price < ⅔ net current asset value, debt < book equity, current ratio > 2, etc.) are historically important but Damodaran flags the net-current-asset-value screen as "too restrictive" today ([Passive Value investing](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/06/passive-value-investing-screening-for.html), 2012-06).
- Intrinsic "cheap" PE threshold for a no-growth, full-payout firm = 1/cost of equity (worked example: cost of equity ≈ 8% in June 2012 → threshold PE ≈ 12.5).
- A simple over/underreaction check on a bank stock in crisis: back out implied ROE from `Price/Book = (ROE − g)/(Cost of equity − g)`; if implied ROE is well below the pre-crisis actual ROE even after generously discounting for the bad news, the selloff looks like overreaction (JPMorgan 2012 example below).
- "You have to bring something uncommon to the table to take something away" — a repeated heuristic for judging whether *you* have earned the right to deviate from the index: an edge must be scarce and not easily replicable, not just "I did my homework" ([Herding behavior](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/herding-behavior-why-so-what-and-what.html), 2011-01; [Value Investing: Where is the beef?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/06/value-investing-where-is-beef.html), 2012-06; [Active Investing: Seeking the Elusive Edge](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/12/active-investing-seeking-elusive-edge.html), 2016-12).
- Four candidate personal "edges" for an active investor in a flattened information world (2016): (a) being in sync with your own client/capital — e.g., investing only your own money; (b) selling liquidity to a panicked market when you value liquidity less than others do; (c) exploiting a different tax profile than the marginal investor; (d) a "big picture" cross-market/cross-time perspective that specialists lack.
- Rather than being an absolutist on margin of safety, treat a cash-heavy portfolio as a warning sign that your margin-of-safety bar is set too high and too few opportunities are passing ([Value Investing III](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/10/value-investing-iii-requiem-rebirth-or.html), 2020-10).
- **Self-check for growth/value bias ("snowmen and shovels"):** extreme growth investors see every company's snowstorm as a chance to build a snowman (dazzled by upside, ignore survival risk); extreme value investors only reach for the shovel (demand worst-case cash flows plus a heavy margin of safety, so they never find growth "bargains"). Both sides are half-right — some richly-priced growth companies really will be big winners, and most won't — so periodically audit your own assumptions in a valuation and ask whether you're tilting all-snowman or all-shovel, then deliberately correct toward the other side ([Snowmen and Shovels](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/01/snowmen-and-shovels-investing-lessons.html), 2012-01).
- On leverage and conviction: leverage should track conviction, but "even if you are a good investor... using debt to turbocharge returns can shrink your time horizon by forcing liquidation before the market corrects" — evaluate any levered position by asking whether the strategy survives a bad month, not just whether the thesis is right ([Situational Awareness Blow-up](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/08/the-situational-awareness-blow-up.html), 2026-08).

## 4. Myths and mistakes Damodaran calls out

- **"Beat-the-market" strategies that work on paper vanish in practice — the main culprit is transaction costs, not lack of genuine market inefficiency.** Average active manager underperforms the index by 1–1.5%/year, stable across four decades, with no persistence between winners and losers ([Transactions Costs and Beating the Market](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/02/transactions-costs-and-beating-market.html), 2010-02). The academic "loser stock" reversal effect came almost entirely from sub-$1 stocks with 20–25% bid-ask spreads; remove them and the excess return disappears. Real-world casualties: James Rea's Graham-based fund (1982–late 1990s) ranked in the bottom 20% of funds; Value Line's own fund underperformed its celebrated hypothetical/backtested rankings.
- **Buffett mythology vs. reality**: he does use DCF-style thinking despite eschewing the jargon; he implicitly adjusts for risk via conservative ("certainty equivalent") cash-flow estimates rather than betas; and he has real information/access advantages ordinary investors lack (e.g., preferred-stock deal terms from Goldman Sachs during the crisis) ([Buffett: Man or Myth](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/03/buffett-man-or-myth.html), 2009-03). His signature skill is separating "great companies" from "great investments" — Burlington Northern (2009) was a boring, low-growth, low-ROIC railroad, yet a good investment at the right price ([Bad companies and good investments](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/11/bad-companies-and-good-investments.html), 2009-11). Damodaran also directly rebuts Buffett/Munger's 2009 dismissal of "modern" academic finance and 2011 dismissal of Black-Scholes for long-dated options — the model's known flaws (undervaluing deep-OTM, overvaluing illiquid options) are fixable with proper inputs/adjustments, not solved by rejecting quantitative valuation altogether ([Buffett and Munger... Shock value!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/05/buffett-and-munger-shock-value.html), 2009-05; [Buffett and Black-Scholes](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/buffett-and-black-scholes.html), 2011-02). In the 2010s, even Buffett's stock picking delivered average returns — Berkshire's price-to-book fell from 1.54 (start of 2010, vs. a 1.10 US insurance-industry average) to 1.27, *below* the 1.47 peer average (start of 2020), evidence the market had stopped paying a "stock-picker premium" for him ([Value Investing II](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/10/value-investing-ii-tough-times-for.html), 2020-10).
- **"Past performance is no guarantee" disclaimers are ignored — chasing recent winners is systematic.** Permanent Portfolio fund grew from ~$50M to ~$5B (2007→2010) mostly on a lucky gold/cash asset-allocation tilt, not stock-picking skill ([Past performance is no guarantee...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/past-performance-is-no-guarantee-of.html), 2010-09).
- **Value investing has "lost its way" by becoming rigid, ritualistic, and righteous** — treating tangible-asset/dividend screens as immutable law, treating Graham/Buffett texts as scripture, and treating deviation from value doctrine as moral failure ([Value Investing III](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/10/value-investing-iii-requiem-rebirth-or.html), 2020-10). Damodaran's prescribed fixes: separate price from value explicitly; treat uncertainty as a feature to model (probability distributions, decision trees, simulation), not a reason to avoid growth/young companies; stop treating margin of safety as a *substitute* for a risk measure — you still need a risk-adjusted discount rate before a margin of safety means anything; distrust accounting numbers more given aggressive earnings management and R&D's mistreatment as an expense; diversify *and* pick stocks — it's not either/or; and drop the sense of entitlement to superior returns for "doing your homework."
- **The small-cap premium and the pure "value premium" (low P/B) are not reliable, time-invariant facts — they are period-dependent, and both broke down after 1980/2010 respectively.** Small caps earned ~3.47%/yr more than the market 1927–2019 but *0.10% less* 1980–2019 ([A Viral Market Update VIII](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/05/a-viral-market-update-viii-crisis-test.html), 2020-05). "Once bad valuation practices, once embedded in the status quo, are very difficult to remove" — Damodaran explicitly criticizes continued use of small-cap premiums in appraisal/valuation practice as having "no basis in the data."
- **Active management collectively *cannot* beat the market — it's arithmetic, not opinion.** If X% of institutional equity money is passive (tracking the index net of ~0.11% costs) and the rest is active, the active pool's *pre-cost* return must equal the market's, so post-cost it must underperform; more than half of active managers must therefore lag the index in any period ([Active Investing: Rest in Peace or Resurgent Force?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/12/active-investing-rest-in-peace-or.html), 2016-12). No style sub-group (value, growth, any market cap, any country) escapes this collectively, and consistency is essentially absent — quartile rankings show almost no persistence except elevated movement from 1st-quartile to 4th-quartile (risk-seekers bouncing between extremes).
- **"Smart money" is mostly a self-serving myth, not evidence of skill.** Hedge funds are more likely to be "information traders" than mutual funds because of asymmetric compensation (2-and-20 gives a costless call option on outsized bets), unconstrained long/short mandates, leverage, and a more demanding/faster-moving client base — this explains their overrepresentation in insider-trading scandals, not superior average skill; in aggregate they match or underperform mutual funds after fees ([The insider trading scandal](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/11/insider-trading-scandal-thoughts-about.html), 2010-11). Herding is common precisely because most managers lack a genuine competitive edge and know it ([Herding behavior](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/herding-behavior-why-so-what-and-what.html), 2011-01).
- **Alternative investments' correlation-based diversification pitch is often an artifact of stale, appraisal-based (not market) pricing, and their alpha has largely faded as AUM has scaled.** Private-asset correlations with public markets look artificially low because of pricing lags, and rise sharply during real crises (2008, 2020) — exactly when diversification is needed most ([The (Uncertain) Payoff from Alternative Investments](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/06/the-uncertain-payoff-from-alternative.html), 2025-06). Hedge-fund alpha fell to roughly zero by ~2009 and hedge-fund/public-market correlation rose from 0.65 (1990s) to 0.87 (2010s) per Sullivan's study; private-equity returns that beat public markets 1998–2007 have converged toward public-market returns since. Damodaran's conclusion: "the average venture capital, private equity or hedge fund manager is now no better or worse than the average mutual fund manager," while 2-and-20-style fees remain a large, near-guaranteed drag.
- **Investment "conviction" is not automatically a virtue — it can be indistinguishable from overconfidence until tested by leverage.** The Situational Awareness hedge fund (Leo Aschenbrenner) rose almost 450% through late June 2026 (about 367% from Aug 7, 2025) on a maximally leveraged, concentrated AI-winners/losers bet, then lost >two-thirds of its public-equity value and was forced to liquidate to Citadel within four weeks in July 2026 — not because the AI thesis was necessarily wrong, but because "combining a macro story... with maximal leverage creates a time bomb" that truncates the time horizon needed for the thesis to play out ([The Situational Awareness Blow-up](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/08/the-situational-awareness-blow-up.html), 2026-08). Damodaran's parallel to Long-Term Capital Management: experience and Nobel-caliber pedigree don't inoculate against overleveraged conviction.

- **"Regulatory crackdowns on 'Insider Trading 2.0' (analyst-survey access, HFT premium data feeds) protect small investors."** Damodaran argues NY AG Schneiderman's 2014 actions against banks' analyst surveys and HFT's paid-for millisecond data access misdiagnose the problem: neither fits the legal definition of insider trading (no material non-public *company* information changed hands), the real issue (if any) is a *fiduciary-duty* breach by exchanges/data vendors monetizing data they didn't generate, and — citing the precedent of Eliot Spitzer's early-2000s crackdown on conflicted sell-side research, which left research "no less conflicted... or of higher quality" a decade later — Damodaran predicts small investors gain nothing and may lose legitimate access to information ([Impactful information is not always insider](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/03/impactful-information-is-not-always.html), 2014-03).
- **"A stable earnings-surprise-to-price-reaction relationship is a durable, tradeable pattern."** The clean decile-by-decile pattern (positive surprise → proportionally positive price move) Damodaran documented in 2012 had, by 2026, weakened into a relationship where "a significant subset of positive surprises" produce price *drops* — because public dissemination of consensus estimates and revisions increasingly pre-prices the surprise before it's announced, and because firms' own gaming of the beat has become common knowledge investors discount ([Earnings surprises, price reaction and value](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/07/earnings-surprises-price-reaction-and.html), 2012-07; [Information Timing and Release](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html), 2026-07).

## 5. How Damodaran's views evolved (dated)

- **2009-05 → 2011-02**: Openly combative toward Buffett/Munger's public dismissals of "modern" academic finance and derivatives math, accusing them of "shock value" rhetoric aimed at anti-academic sentiment; by 2011 explicitly vows to "take every macro suggestion [Buffett] makes and do the opposite" regarding Buffett's excursions outside his stock-picking competence.
- **2009-07 → 2011-03**: Self-described shift from behavioral-finance *skeptic* to accepting the evidence base — folding it into corporate finance decision-making broadly, while maintaining that core intrinsic valuation mechanics are largely unaffected; explicitly credits the accumulation of evidence for the change of mind.
- **2012-06 (5-part value investing series)**: At this point still largely sympathetic-but-analytical toward value investing as a legitimate, evidence-backed philosophy (screens do show statistically higher returns for low PE/PBV before costs) while already flagging that "no group of investors has a monopoly on investment virtue."
- **2016-08 → 2016-12**: Sharpened critique of mean reversion as the "elephant in the room" assumption behind both market timing and stock selection, tracing Damodaran's personal "awakening to the dangers of mean reversion... to the 2008 crisis," and connects this to why value/momentum screening payoffs have been decaying as the "investment world gets flatter."
- **2020-05 → 2020-10 (COVID crisis test + 3-part Value Investing retrospective)**: Marks the most decisive break from traditional value-investing orthodoxy. Pre-COVID, Damodaran still treated the 2010s value underperformance as possibly cyclical/Fed-driven; the COVID crisis data (lowest-PE and highest-dividend-yield stocks were the *worst* performers, Feb–May 2020) convinced Damodaran that the underperformance reflects structural economic change (book value increasingly meaningless outside financials, R&D accounting distorting growth-company books), not a "passing phase." The conclusion, now stated plainly: value investing "has lost its way" and needs to formally separate price from value, embrace uncertainty/probabilistic tools, and stop conflating "screens" with genuine intrinsic valuation.
- **2012-07 → 2026-07 (the earnings/guidance game)**: In 2012 Damodaran treats earnings-surprise-driven price drift as a live, if modest, tradeable pattern and "whispered earnings" as a curiosity (Apple beating whispers 42 of 56 quarters). By 2026, writing amid an SEC proposal to move from quarterly to semi-annual reporting and a new Fed chair (Warsh) signaling less forward guidance, the earnings game is generalized into a broader "information timing and release" policy question, documents that the earnings-surprise/price link has empirically weakened as the game became more transparent, and lands on a "less guidance, less disclosure-bloat, but keep the reporting cadence" compromise applied symmetrically to corporate earnings reports and central-bank communication.
- **2025-04 → 2026-08**: Increasingly explicit about Damodaran's own contrarian process (naming personal limit-buy orders on BYD, Mercado Libre, Palantir in the April 2025 tariff selloff) and about conviction/leverage as the central risk lever in modern active investing, reflecting a world of AI-driven macro narratives and hedge funds built around single-theme, highly leveraged bets.

## 7. Cases and examples

- **Volkswagen short squeeze, Oct 2008**: Porsche's low free float (>60% held by non-sellers) plus a surprise stake-increase announcement briefly made VW the world's largest company by market cap, costing shorting hedge funds ~$20bn; Damodaran's takeaway — "you live by the sword, you die by the sword," no sympathy for hedge funds outmaneuvered at their own game ([Another strange incident](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/10/another-strange-incident-in-market-full.html), 2008-10).
- **Burlington Northern (Berkshire Hathaway acquisition), Nov 2009**: a "boring," low-ROIC, mature railroad — proof that "great company" and "great investment" are separate judgments; Buffett's rationale was price, not a macro bet on the US economy ([Bad companies and good investments](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/11/bad-companies-and-good-investments.html), 2009-11).
- **JPMorgan Chase 2012 trading-loss selloff**: a >$30bn market-cap decline on a reported $2bn loss; implied-ROE back-of-envelope (P/B formula) suggested the market was pricing in a bigger ROE hit than the fundamentals justified — presented as a live example of the "collateral damage / comeback bet" contrarian framework ([Contrarian Value Investing](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/06/contrarian-investing-going-against-flow.html), 2012-06).
- **Nokia vs. RIM, 2012**: framed as "long-odds option" plays — proprietary tech, low remaining equity value (so limited further downside), operating in a rapidly shifting industry; Damodaran favored Nokia over RIM on magnitude of price drop, debt-driven leverage on any asset-value recovery, and Eurozone-driven extra optionality.
- **Vale and Lukoil, Nov 2014 → Apr 2015**: Damodaran's own real-money "go where it is darkest" contrarian bet, revisited five months later with full transparency — Vale's thesis got *worse* (iron ore -31%, Brazilian real -15%, Petrobras scandal spillover) yet it was still judged 42% undervalued and held/added; Lukoil moved from undervalued to slightly overvalued and the choice was to hold rather than buy more ([The Search for Investment Serenity](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/04/the-search-for-investment-serenity-look.html), 2015-04).
- **NY AG "Insider Trading 2.0" (2014)**: Eric Schneiderman's crackdown on banks letting preferred clients see analyst-sentiment surveys early, and on HFT firms paying for millisecond-early data access, packaged as a new insider-trading doctrine — Damodaran's counter-analysis concludes neither practice involves company insider information, both are, at most, fiduciary-duty issues (data vendors/exchanges monetizing data they didn't produce), and cites Spitzer's earlier, largely ineffectual research-conflict crackdown as the base-rate precedent for expecting little investor benefit ([Impactful information is not always insider](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/03/impactful-information-is-not-always.html), 2014-03).
- **Apple's "earnings game," 2012 vs. 2026**: in July 2012, Apple's whispered-earnings bar (consensus EPS $10.35, whispered $11.02) illustrated how serial beaters get re-priced by the market's own adaptive expectations; by 2026, Apple's consensus forecasts and revisions were publicly tracked via Yahoo Finance/Zacks, and FactSet data on S&P 500 Q2 2026 reports showed a disproportionately large number of positive surprises, with markets readjusting to routine beats (whispered earnings) — a paired before/after case of the earnings game ([Earnings surprises](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/07/earnings-surprises-price-reaction-and.html), 2012-07; [Information Timing and Release](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html), 2026-07).
- **Apple earnings, Jan 26 2016**: beat EPS but missed revenue/iPhone units and lost ~$30bn in market cap — used to show that different investors (dividend-focused vs. cash-flow-focused) can reasonably read the *same* report as overreaction or as legitimately consequential, depending on which cash-flow driver they weight ([A Violent Earnings Season](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/02/a-violent-earnings-season-pricing-and.html), 2016-02).
- **Yale endowment / David Swensen model**: early and aggressive alternative-asset allocation (PE, hedge funds) drove Yale's outperformance for much of the 2000s–2010s, held up as the model other endowments chased — until Yale itself announced in June 2025 it would sell billions in private-equity holdings after years of underperformance, which Damodaran treats as a signal the "easy money" era in alternatives is over ([Alternative Investments](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/06/the-uncertain-payoff-from-alternative.html), 2025-06).
- **Situational Awareness fund / Leopold "Leo" Aschenbrenner, 2024–2026**: 25-year-old ex-OpenAI researcher's hedge fund, built entirely around a leveraged long-AI-winners/short-AI-losers macro bet, including a private Anthropic stake; rose ~450% then collapsed >67% in its public book over four weeks (July 2026) and was liquidated, with Citadel buying the public holdings — Damodaran's central teaching case for the risks of conviction + leverage + concentration, and for "humble money" over "smart money" ([The Situational Awareness Blow-up](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/08/the-situational-awareness-blow-up.html), 2026-08).
- **BYD, Mercado Libre, Palantir limit orders, April 2025**: Damodaran's personal application of "opportunistic contrarianism" during the tariff-driven selloff — pre-existing watchlist names Damodaran had always wanted to own, bought via standing limit orders when the crisis pushed price toward (not below) the pre-set valuation, not a re-engineered valuation to fit the new price ([Buy the Dip](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/04/buy-dip-draw-and-dangers-of-contrarian.html), 2025-04).
