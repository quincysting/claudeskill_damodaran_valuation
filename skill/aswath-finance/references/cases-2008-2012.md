## Amazon
- **Context**: December 17, 2011, prompted by a *New York Times* piece (James Stewart) praising Jeff Bezos for long-term thinking and arguing markets punished Amazon short-sightedly after its October 25, 2011 earnings report ([Do markets punish long term thinking? Amazon as a case study](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/12/do-markets-punish-long-term-thinking.html), 2011-12).
- **Story / narrative**: Damodaran pushes back on the "market is short-term" framing — Amazon's own history (revered and reviled repeatedly since 1998) argues against it.
- **Key inputs / facts cited**: Market cap rose from $4.55B (2001) to $82B (2011), a cumulative 1300% return for a 2001 buyer; peak dot-com cap was only $30B. In November 2011 Amazon traded at 96x trailing earnings and 2x trailing revenues vs. a median retail-sector PE of ~15 and EV/revenue of 0.8. Damodaran had valued Amazon nearly every year since 1998: overvalued by ~60% in Jan 2000, undervalued by ~50% a year later; overvalued in 7 of 12 years, undervalued in 5. Since 2009, price rose 300%+ ($54 to $200+). For the ~$200/share pre-earnings price to be justified, Amazon would need revenues over $200B in 10 years, pre-tax margin rising from 2.5% to 4%, and 70%+ return on new capital.
- **Result**: No single "value per share" given; verdict is that Amazon "does not look cheap" and the market is "still much too optimistic" (at ~$200 just before the October earnings report it was "valued to perfection and beyond"), even granting Bezos's long-term focus and CEO quality ([Do markets punish long term thinking?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/12/do-markets-punish-long-term-thinking.html), 2011-12).
- **Lesson**: A great CEO and a long-term focus do not make a stock cheap; growth only creates value if delivered at a reasonable cost, and a stock is "cheap" only if price reflects that cost, not the narrative.
- Sources: [Do markets punish long term thinking? Amazon as a case study](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/12/do-markets-punish-long-term-thinking.html) (2011-12).

## Apple
- **Context**: A five-part 2011–2012 arc following Apple's cash pile, capital-return decision, Damodaran's personal sale of the stock, the iPhone franchise, and a year-end retrospective; Damodaran was a stockholder from 1997 until April 2012.
- **Story / narrative**: Cash is a neutral asset (earns riskless rate, should be judged neutrally) unless a "stupidity discount" is warranted by a bad capital-allocation record; Apple's record argued for trust. The later arc turns on dividend clienteles and momentum crowding the stock, not on valuation.
- **Jan 19, 2011 — cash post**: Apple's cash balance ~$50B, market cap ~thirty-fold decade rise, ROIC >30%. Apple's cost of capital ~9%; cash earns the T-bill rate (0.16% then), so cash is a neutral, not "wasted," asset. Verdict: no reason to demand a payout; "do no harm" (keep investing selectively) is the best scenario ([How much cash is too much? Looking at Apple](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/how-much-cash-is-too-much-looking-at.html), 2011-01).
- **Mar 1, 2012 — bias/cash/dividends post**: Market cap just crossed $500B, cash at $100B. DCF inputs: 8% revenue growth for 5 years, 30% target margin (conservative vs. recent history) → **value of $710/share** (first posted as $716, corrected to $710) against a price of $545, i.e., ~23% undervalued (price and gap as stated in the [Apr 3, 2012 post](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/apple-holding-versus-folding.html), 2012-04). Estimated optimal debt ratio 40–50% vs. actual -20% net debt ratio; using debt could add ~5% to value (~$20–25B). Recommends a large buyback (not a dividend), capped at a price multiple (~16x earnings), Berkshire-style, keeping ~$15–20B cash on hand ([Apple: Thoughts on bias, value, excess cash and dividends](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/03/apple-thoughts-on-bias-value-excess.html), 2012-03).
- **Apr 3, 2012 — sale post**: Apple announced a smaller-than-recommended return ($10B buyback + ~$10/share regular dividend, ~$10B/year) rather than the large buyback urged. Still valued at $710 (price ~$620, ~13% undervalued at the time) — Damodaran sold anyway, explicitly **not** for valuation reasons, but because Apple's stockholder base had become a volatile mix of momentum traders, institutional "hot money," and a new dividend clientele with conflicting demands ([Apple: Know when to hold 'em, know when to fold 'em](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/apple-holding-versus-folding.html), 2012-04).
- **Apr 6, 2012 — postscript**: Defends selling below intrinsic value as consistent with "intrinsic value investing" broadly defined — argues dividend-clientele mismatches (citing Lucent as a cautionary tale) can destroy value even when operations are fine, if management gets stampeded into inconsistent policy ([Emotions, Intrinsic value and Dividend Clienteles](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/emotions-intrinsic-value-and-dividend.html), 2012-04).
- **Aug 29, 2012 — iPhone franchise valuation**: Following the Samsung patent win and market cap of $623.5B. Inputs: 21% after-tax operating margin maintained; smartphone market growth 6% compounded for 10 years, fading to GDP growth; 2-year product life cycle with 50% of after-tax income reinvested in cycle year 2; switching ratio 5% of iPhone users lost per cycle vs. 10% gained from competitors; cost of capital 11% (90th percentile risk). **Value of iPhone franchise: $307 billion** (~55% of Apple's total business value), 3.39x revenues, 16.17x net income. Sensitivities given: margin drop to 15% → $219B; life cycle to 1 year → roughly halves value; neutral switching ratio → $255B ([Apple's Crown Jewel: Valuing the iPhone Franchise](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/apples-crown-jewel-valuing-iphone.html), 2012-08).
- **Dec 27, 2012 — year-in-review**: Recaps the full-year arc and updates the DCF to **$609/share** (down from $710 in March), against a price testing $500 — still calls Apple undervalued and places a personal buy-limit order at $500. Lower revenue growth but similar dollar revenues; higher cost of capital (more Asia dependence/risk); conservative one-time tax hit of $18.4B assumed on trapped cash ([The year in review: Apple's universe?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/12/the-year-in-review-apples-universe.html), 2012-12).
- **Revisits (2013)**: Jan 27, 2013 — after a post-earnings crash to $440/share, reiterates the $609 estimate (barely moved) and runs a simulation showing ~90% probability of undervaluation ([Are you a value investor? Take the Apple test](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/01/are-you-value-investor-apple-test.html), 2013-01). Feb 7, 2013 — explains why 90%-confidence-undervalued does not mean "bet the house" (pricing risk is separate from valuation confidence) ([Back to Apple: Thoughts on value, price and the confidence gap](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/02/apple-redux-thoughts-on-value-price-and.html), 2013-02). Apr 30, 2013 — updates value to **$588/share**, ~3.5% below the January $609 estimate ([Apple: News, Noise and Value](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/04/apple-calm-after-storm.html), 2013-04).
- **Lesson**: Cash is a neutral asset unless management's track record earns a "stupidity discount"; a stock can be simultaneously undervalued on fundamentals and a bad personal holding because its shareholder base (dividend seekers vs. growth seekers vs. momentum traders) is internally inconsistent — value and pricing are different games, and even 90%-confidence undervaluation does not eliminate pricing risk.
- Sources: [How much cash is too much? Looking at Apple](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/how-much-cash-is-too-much-looking-at.html) (2011-01), [Apple: Thoughts on bias, value, excess cash and dividends](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/03/apple-thoughts-on-bias-value-excess.html) (2012-03), [Apple: Know when to hold 'em, know when to fold 'em](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/apple-holding-versus-folding.html) (2012-04), [Emotions, Intrinsic value and Dividend Clienteles](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/emotions-intrinsic-value-and-dividend.html) (2012-04), [Apple's Crown Jewel: Valuing the iPhone Franchise](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/apples-crown-jewel-valuing-iphone.html) (2012-08), [The year in review: Apple's universe?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/12/the-year-in-review-apples-universe.html) (2012-12), [Are you a value investor? Take the Apple test](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/01/are-you-value-investor-apple-test.html) (2013-01), [Back to Apple](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/02/apple-redux-thoughts-on-value-price-and.html) (2013-02), [Apple: News, Noise and Value](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/04/apple-calm-after-storm.html) (2013-04).

## Disney
- **Context**: September 17, 2010, prompted by ex-CEO Michael Eisner's forthcoming book on "great partnerships" ([Checks and Balances: Eisner and Disney](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/checks-and-balances-eisner-and-disney.html), 2010-09).
- **Story / narrative**: Not a valuation post — a governance case study. Disney is Damodaran's long-running teaching case (used across three editions/15 years of the corporate finance textbook).
- **Key facts cited**: Eisner + COO Frank Wells turned around a moribund Disney (aging parks, weak animation) by 1994; Wells died in a helicopter crash in 1994, removing Eisner's main internal check. Eisner then packed the board with compliant directors; cited mistakes: buying Capital Cities/ABC for $18B when only ESPN was wanted (could have been bought alone far more cheaply), and passing on buying Pixar early for "millions" rather than paying billions a decade later. By 2003 stockholders were in "full revolt" as earnings plateaued and the stock underperformed the market.
- **Result**: No valuation given (not a pricing post); verdict is that Eisner's later-tenure record was damaging and that a stronger board could have constrained his "manias and paranoia" without needing to turn a good manager into one — merely keeping him from going "over to the dark side."
- **Lesson**: Boards/checks and balances matter not because they can turn bad managers into good ones, but because even talented, well-intentioned managers develop unconstrained weaknesses over time that eventually overwhelm their successes.
- Sources: [Checks and Balances: Eisner and Disney](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/checks-and-balances-eisner-and-disney.html) (2010-09).

## Facebook
- **Context**: Seven posts spanning January 2011 (Goldman's pre-IPO stake) through August 2012 (post-IPO crash to ~$19), tracking the same DCF model updated as new S-1/earnings data arrived.
- **Story / narrative**: A "winner take all" social-advertising platform compared throughout to Google's growth/margin trajectory; the central tension across posts is pricing (momentum, IPO mechanics, insider behavior) vs. valuation (DCF).
- **Jan 5, 2011 — Goldman stake post**: Goldman's $500M investment implied a $50B valuation; Damodaran argues single-transaction extrapolation is unreliable once you net out side benefits to Goldman (IPO underwriting, private banking, client access) — a fairer implied value could be closer to $30B if those side benefits are worth ~$200M in present value. No independent value estimate given yet ([The Facebook Valuation!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/facebook-valuation.html), 2011-01).
- **Feb 4, 2012 — options/tax post**: Not a valuation, but flags that Zuckerberg's pre-IPO option exercise (~$5B) would trigger ~$2B in personal taxes and let Facebook claim ~$500M in tax refunds — used to critique proposed "Facebook tax" legislation on stock-option deductions ([Options and Taxes: Is a "Facebook" tax next?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/02/options-and-taxes-is-facebook-tax-next.html), 2012-02).
- **Feb 16, 2012 — S-1 valuation**: 2011 revenue $3,711M (up 88% from $1,974M in 2010, which was up 150% from $777M in 2009); pre-tax operating income $1,756M; book equity $5,228M; total debt (incl. capitalized leases) $1,174M. Assumptions: 40% compounded revenue growth for 5 years fading to 2.01% (risk-free rate) by year 10, modeled on Google's post-IPO revenue path; operating margin fading from 45% to 35% over a decade (vs. Google's ~31%); reinvestment of $1.50 revenue per $1 capital; cost of capital 11.42% fading to 8%. **Value of operating assets: $71,240M; equity value $71,578M; value per share $29.05** (~$28 for Class A after a small non-voting discount) against a market price talk of $75–100B. Simulation gave a median of ~$70B, 10% chance of >$117B, 10% chance of <$43B ([The IPO of the decade? My valuation of Facebook](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/02/ipo-of-decade-my-valuation-of-facebook.html), 2012-02).
- **Feb 23, 2012 — IPO-pop mechanics post**: Not a valuation update; a cautionary piece on the "IPO pop" strategy — rationing at the offer price means you get proportionally less of the most underpriced deals; underpricing is largest for the smallest offerings (a headwind for Facebook's very large deal); post-IPO 5-year returns lag non-IPO stocks ([Facebook: Playing the "IPO pop" game?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/02/facebook-playing-ipo-pop-game.html), 2012-02).
- **May 17, 2012 — pre-IPO update**: Updated assumptions after Instagram/Tagtile/Glancee acquisitions: revenue growth raised to 45% compounded (to $56B), margin lowered to 30%, reinvestment $1.25 revenue per $1 capital → **value stays in the $70–80B range** (vs. the February $29/share, ~$72B equity estimate); calls it the "Field of Dreams" business model (cites the news of GM abandoning Facebook advertising as a cautionary signal) and forecasts a "Goldilocks IPO" (10–15% pop, not a blowout) ([Facebook and "Field of Dreams": Hoodies, Hubris and Hoopla](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/05/facebook-hoodies-hubris-and-hoopla.html), 2012-05).
- **May 23, 2012 — post-IPO post-mortem**: Stock opened up ~12% over the $38 offer price, faded to the offer price by close (propped up by the underwriters), then fell to ~$32 within days. Distinguishes "pricing" (multiples, demand/supply, technical analysis) from "valuation" (DCF); attributes the crash to a momentum shift triggered by the bankers'/company's own hubris (raising the offer price late, insiders selling immediately) rather than to malice or leaked information ([Facebook: Sowing the wind, reaping the whirlwind](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/05/facebook-sowing-wind-reaping-whirlwind.html), 2012-05).
- **Aug 20, 2012 — "face plant" update**: Stock closed at $19 (~half the IPO price) after a disappointing earnings report (share-comp-driven operating loss). Revised assumptions: 2022 revenue estimate cut ~10% from May, operating margin cut from 35% to 32%, sales-to-capital ratio cut from 1.50 to 1.20. **Updated value: $23.94/share**, only ~10% below the May estimate despite the price collapse. Simulation: ~80% probability stock is undervalued at $19, ~85% at $18. Sets a personal limit buy order at **$18** (calls it "getting close" to a buy, not quite there, given corporate-governance concerns) ([Facebook face plant: Time to friend the company?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/facebook-face-plant-time-to-friend.html), 2012-08).
- **Revisit (2013)**: July 31, 2013 — stock briefly touched the $38 IPO price 15 months later; recaps the Feb-2012 $68B ($29/share-equivalent) valuation as the anchor for a fresh update ([Market Mood Swings: Facebook Euphoria](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/07/facebook-hits-ipo-price-mood-swings-in.html), 2013-07).
- **Lesson**: A single-transaction "implied valuation" (Goldman's stake) is unreliable once side payments are netted out; pricing (multiples, momentum, banker mechanics) and valuation (DCF) are different processes that can diverge sharply around an IPO, and a "Goldilocks" pricing job by underwriters does not prevent a subsequent momentum-driven crash.
- Sources: [The Facebook Valuation!](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/facebook-valuation.html) (2011-01), [Options and Taxes](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/02/options-and-taxes-is-facebook-tax-next.html) (2012-02), [The IPO of the decade?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/02/ipo-of-decade-my-valuation-of-facebook.html) (2012-02), [Facebook: Playing the "IPO pop" game?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/02/facebook-playing-ipo-pop-game.html) (2012-02), [Hoodies, Hubris and Hoopla](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/05/facebook-hoodies-hubris-and-hoopla.html) (2012-05), [Sowing the wind, reaping the whirlwind](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/05/facebook-sowing-wind-reaping-whirlwind.html) (2012-05), [Facebook face plant](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/facebook-face-plant-time-to-friend.html) (2012-08), [Market Mood Swings: Facebook Euphoria](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/07/facebook-hits-ipo-price-mood-swings-in.html) (2013-07).

## GE (General Electric)
- **Context**: September 25, 2008, days into the bank-bailout debate, when GE suspended its stock buyback program citing declining earnings and GE Capital worries ([GE's aborted buybacks...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/09/ges-aborted-buybacks.html), 2008-09).
- **Story / narrative**: A short, three-point commentary on buybacks as a policy tool, not a valuation.
- **Key facts cited**: S&P 500 companies returned twice as much cash via buybacks as dividends in the prior year, for a total shareholder yield of 5.34% (1.9% from dividends, the rest from buybacks). Microsoft had a $40B buyback program announced around the same time.
- **Result**: No value estimate. Observations only: (1) buybacks give firms flexibility to cut back without the negative signal a dividend cut would send — market reaction to GE's suspension was muted; (2) announced buyback programs should be discounted since many are never fully executed; (3) the aggregate buyback number's resilience would be a useful gauge of whether the equity market could sustain the 2008 crisis shock.
- **Lesson**: Buybacks are valued by markets as more reversible/flexible than dividends, so suspending one carries far less negative signaling than a dividend cut — but investors should treat announced buyback programs skeptically until executed.
- Sources: [GE's aborted buybacks...](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/09/ges-aborted-buybacks.html) (2008-09).

## Goldman Sachs
- **Context**: Two posts, two years apart — the SEC's Abacus CDO indictment (April 2010) and Greg Smith's public resignation op-ed (March 2012).
- **Story / narrative**: Not valuations; governance/ethics commentary defending Goldman on narrow legal grounds while criticizing investment-banking incentive structures more broadly.
- **Apr 30, 2010 — Abacus indictment**: The SEC's case rested on (1) Goldman allegedly misrepresenting that John Paulson's fund was a buyer rather than the seller in the Abacus CDO, and (2) the claim that Goldman knew the housing market would collapse and exploited clients (evidenced via Fabrice Tourre's emails). Damodaran argues the case is "selective prosecution" using 20/20 hindsight; the post finds no explicit mention of Paulson's role in the sales pitchbook reviewed, and argues that internal Goldman views on the housing market were not monolithic, so one trader's bearish emails don't establish firm-wide intent. Concludes Goldman likely took advantage of "naive" clients (bad practice, common in trading) but that this is not illegal ([The Goldman indictment](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/04/goldman-indictment.html), 2010-04).
- **Mar 18, 2012 — Greg Smith resignation**: Smith's NYT op-ed accused Goldman of contempt for clients ("muppets"). Damodaran argues the client relationship is "mutually exploitative" (institutional clients aren't naive either), and attributes declining client focus to three industry-wide forces: deal-shopping by clients, over-specialization in narrow financial products, and compensation tied too tightly to deal/trading volume rather than long-term relationships. Recommends Goldman lead a reform of hiring (favor generalists), incentive structure, and client selection ([Greg Smith on Goldman: An indictment of investment banking?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/03/greg-smith-on-goldman-indictment-of.html), 2012-03).
- **Result**: No valuation in either post.
- **Lesson**: Distinguish legal/ethical critique from valuation; "taking advantage of naive counterparties" is a recurring, largely legal feature of trading businesses, and client-banker relationships are two-way exploitative rather than purely predatory.
- Sources: [The Goldman indictment](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/04/goldman-indictment.html) (2010-04), [Greg Smith on Goldman](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/03/greg-smith-on-goldman-indictment-of.html) (2012-03).

## Google
- **Context**: Three posts using Google as the benchmark for growth-company mechanics: dual-class voting structure (May 2011, triggered by the LinkedIn IPO), scaling growth (Oct 2011), and the April 2012 stock split with non-voting shares.
- **Story / narrative**: Google is treated throughout as the reference "successful growth company" against which Facebook, LinkedIn, and Groupon are benchmarked.
- **May 20, 2011 — dual-class structure post**: Explains that Google rewrote the IPO playbook with an auction (instead of a banking syndicate) and unapologetic dual-class shares, listing on NASDAQ when US dual-class use had been limited mostly to media families (NYT, Washington Post) and the NYSE had for decades barred unequal-voting shares; argues voting-share premiums should be larger for badly-managed firms than well-managed ones, and that investors' complacency about Google's structure ("control is worth little because management is good") ignores that management quality can change (cites Cisco's fall from "best managed" a decade earlier). Used to correct a same-week LinkedIn valuation error: initial equity value of ~$2B on 43.31M shares was wrong; correct share count (7.8M Class A + 86.7M Class B) yields **$21.51/share**, dropping to **$20.57/share** with a 5% voting premium adjustment, and to **~$20/share** once ~17M in-the-money options (avg. exercise price $15) are factored in ([Dual share structure: The Google model spreads](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/dual-share-structure-google-model.html), 2011-05).
- **Oct 18, 2011 — growth/value post** (also covers Groupon and Green Mountain Coffee): Uses Google's 2001–2010 revenue history to show that percentage revenue growth decreased every year (except 2009) even at a top-tier growth company, even as dollar growth increases — illustrating that growth naturally decelerates with scale. Notes Google's "price of growth" has risen over time as it needs progressively larger acquisitions to sustain the same growth rate ([Growth and Value: Thoughts on Google, Groupon and Green Mountain](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/10/growth-and-value-thoughts-on-google.html), 2011-10).
- **Apr 13, 2012 — stock split post**: Google announced a 2-for-1 split creating a new non-voting share class, alongside 60% earnings growth and 24% revenue growth. Damodaran's own DCF: pre-split intrinsic value implies the $630/share pre-split price was **~10% overvalued** (and remains ~10% overvalued at $325 post-split, since a split changes nothing fundamental). Argues Larry Page's stated rationale ("protected Google from outside pressures... to sacrifice future opportunities to meet short-term demands") is unconvincing — no decision in Google's history plausibly required the dual-class structure to execute. Frames investor choices as: avoid dual-class stocks entirely, price in a control discount, or accept "what, me worry?" (own it and forfeit the right to later complain) — dubbed the "Kardashian rule" ([Google splits its stock and spits on its stockholders](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/google-splits-its-stock-and-spits-on.html), 2012-04).
- **Result**: No single "buy/sell" verdict on Google stock across these posts; Google's own share price called ~10% overvalued in April 2012.
- **Lesson**: Growth decelerates mechanically with scale even for the best growth companies; dual-class structures should be priced with a control discount that grows with the risk that "good" management could later turn bad; stock splits are value-neutral mechanically but signal (rightly or wrongly) about growth confidence or corporate-control ambitions.
- Sources: [Dual share structure: The Google model spreads](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/dual-share-structure-google-model.html) (2011-05), [Growth and Value: Thoughts on Google, Groupon and Green Mountain](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/10/growth-and-value-thoughts-on-google.html) (2011-10), [Google splits its stock and spits on its stockholders](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/google-splits-its-stock-and-spits-on.html) (2012-04).

## Groupon
- **Context**: Four posts spanning the pre-IPO buildup (Oct/Nov 2011) through the post-IPO collapse (Aug 2012), all using the same evolving DCF.
- **Story / narrative**: A daily-deals marketplace with a thin moat; Damodaran's central concern throughout is the absence of durable barriers to entry.
- **Oct 18, 2011**: Flags accounting controversy over whether customer-acquisition costs should be capitalized, and the open question of whether customer lifetime value is falling as the company chases more elusive customers against Amazon/LivingSocial competition (part of the joint Google/Groupon/Green Mountain post) ([Growth and Value](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/10/growth-and-value-thoughts-on-google.html), 2011-10).
- **Nov 2, 2011 — S-1 valuation**: Trailing-12-month figures from the S-1 filing. Revenue grew from $312M (2010) to $1,290M (TTM 2011), >300% growth. Assumptions: 50% compounded revenue growth for 5 years fading to 2% by year 10 (year-10 revenue ~$25B); operating margin improving to 23% by year 10 (staying negative through year 6); cost of capital 15% initially (beta 2, risk-free 2%, ERP 6.5%) fading to 8% mature; 0% assumed failure probability; return on capital of 10% in perpetuity after year 10. **Value of operating assets: $9.73B**; PV of 10-year cash flows is *negative* $5.4B (implying future dilution from equity raises). After adding IPO proceeds ($478.8M) and cash ($243.9M), netting options (18.4M options, avg. strike $1.11, value $275.53M) → **value per share $14.62** (class A ~$14, class B ~$15.50 after voting-right split), vs. the IPO range of $16–18 ([Are you ready to value Groupon?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/11/are-you-ready-to-value-groupon.html), 2011-11).
- **Nov 4, 2011 — follow-up after the IPO pop**: IPO priced at $20, jumped ~40% intraday. Sensitivity table: even 70% five-year revenue growth (to $64B in year 10) with 23% margin only reaches **$39.81/share**; no scenario with <60% growth reaches $28/share. Monte Carlo simulation (growth uniform 30–70%, margin normal mean 23% sd 4%) gives only a 15% chance of exceeding $28, with an average value around $14. Rebuts the claim that DCF is "inherently conservative" for growth stocks, noting Damodaran's own Amazon valuations were undervalued in 2001 right after being overvalued in 2000 — DCF is contrarian, not conservative ([Following up on Groupon](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/11/following-up-on-groupon.html), 2011-11).
- **Aug 24, 2012 — "Groupon Gloom" post-collapse update**: Stock had fallen to $4.44 from a post-IPO high of $29. Updated base-year figures: TTM revenue $1.965B, operating income $71M. Revised assumptions: revenue growth cut to 25% (year-10 revenue $10.3B, down from $25.4B forecast a year earlier), target margin cut to 12% (from 23%), sales-to-capital ratio cut to 1.25 (from 2.00). **New value per share: $4.07** — down from $14.62 a year prior — described as "at best, a fairly priced stock," with further downside once failure risk is incorporated ([Groupon Gloom: Deal of the day or Death Spiral?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/groupon-gloom-deal-of-day-or-death.html), 2012-08).
- **Result**: Nov 2011: DCF $14.62, "not far off" the low end of the $16–18 IPO range but "over priced" there, and Damodaran would not buy (also citing management's games and the near-zero voting rights). Aug 2012: DCF $4.07 vs. $4.44 price, "at best, a fairly priced stock" as competitive erosion (first-mover advantage eroded by a flood of deal-of-the-day rivals) became visible in the financials.
- **Lesson**: Growth only adds value when protected by competitive advantages/barriers to entry; intrinsic values are not static — they should (and did) fall dramatically once a young company's premise of scalable, defensible growth is empirically undermined, distinct from macro-shock or fraud-driven revaluations.
- Sources: [Growth and Value: Thoughts on Google, Groupon and Green Mountain](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/10/growth-and-value-thoughts-on-google.html) (2011-10), [Are you ready to value Groupon?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/11/are-you-ready-to-value-groupon.html) (2011-11), [Following up on Groupon](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/11/following-up-on-groupon.html) (2011-11), [Groupon Gloom](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/groupon-gloom-deal-of-day-or-death.html) (2012-08).

## HP (Hewlett-Packard)
- **Context**: November 26, 2012, following HP's November 20, 2012 announcement of an $8.8B write-off (of $11.1B paid) for its October 2011 acquisition of UK software firm Autonomy, with $5B of the write-off attributed to alleged accounting fraud at Autonomy ([HP's Deal from Hell: The mark-it-up and write-it-down two-step](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/11/hps-deal-from-hell-mark-it-up-and-write.html), 2012-11).
- **Story / narrative**: A deal-forensics case study decomposing an acquisition price into its constituent premiums and apportioning blame for the value destruction.
- **Key inputs**: Acquisition price $11.1B (£25.50/share). Pre-deal Autonomy market cap ~$5.9B (roughly 6x revenue, 15x earnings — not obviously cheap). Pre-deal book value of equity $2.1B; post-deal (fair-value-adjusted) book value $4.6B. Price breakdown: accounting "write-up" premium over book value = $2,533M; pre-deal market premium over post-deal book value = $1.3B; acquisition/synergy premium = $5.2B. HP's stock lost $15B in market cap (from $58.5B to $43.5B) between deal announcement (Aug 18, 2011) and close (Oct 3, 2011) — the market never endorsed the deal.
- **Fraud-adjustment estimate**: Assuming Autonomy's 2010 revenue was overstated 15% and true margin was the industry-average 31% (vs. reported 36%), Damodaran backs out an implied pre-deal growth rate of 14.25% from the $5.9B market value, then re-runs the DCF with corrected inputs to get an **adjusted pre-deal equity value of $4.2B** — implying the accounting misstatement itself destroyed about **$1.7B** of the original $5.9B market value (not the full $5B HP claimed). Extending proportionally to the synergy premium adds an estimated $749M. **Blame allocation for the $8.8B write-off**: former CEO Leo Apotheker (who pushed the deal) — $4,451M; Autonomy's founder/management and auditor Deloitte (if the fraud claims hold) — $2,449M; current HP management (post-deal deterioration or accounting "kitchen-sinking") — $1,900M.
- **Result**: No going-forward valuation of HP stock; the analysis is a retrospective decomposition of the destroyed value.
- **Lesson**: When decomposing an acquisition write-off, separate the accounting write-up premium, the pre-deal market premium, and the synergy/control premium — and be skeptical of a "the target committed fraud" narrative that conveniently explains the bulk of a write-off, since the market's own pre-deal pricing should already reflect most public misstatements.
- Sources: [HP's Deal from Hell](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/11/hps-deal-from-hell-mark-it-up-and-write.html) (2012-11).

## LinkedIn
- **Context**: Two consecutive posts around LinkedIn's May 19, 2011 IPO, which was trading in the mid-$80s on IPO day (nearly double the $43 offer price).
- **Story / narrative**: Benchmarked against Skype (a prior young-growth valuation exercise) and used to illustrate the mechanics of multi-class share value allocation.
- **May 19, 2011 — IPO-day valuation**: 2010 revenue $243M (up from $120M in 2009, ~100% growth) vs. Skype's 20% growth; LinkedIn already profitable (~$20M pre-tax operating income in 2010) while Skype was not. Assumptions: 50% compounded revenue growth for 5 years fading to 3.5% terminal (2021 revenue ~$5B, benchmarked against Yahoo's $6B); operating margin rising from 8.23% to 15% by 2021 (between Google's and Yahoo's margins); 100% assumed survival probability (little debt, $92M cash pre-IPO). **Value per share: $47** — "at the upper end of the spectrum"; Damodaran initially called the $43 offer price not unreasonable, then struck that line out. Updated after finding the dual-class structure: **value drops into the $20s** once both share classes are properly weighted ([Valuing young growth companies: A postscript on Linkedin](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/valuing-young-growth-companies.html), 2011-05).
- **May 20, 2011 — share-class correction**: Correct share count is 7.8M Class A (IPO shares) + 86.7M Class B (10x voting rights) = 94.5M total, not the 43.31M Yahoo! Finance listed. Equity value of ~$2B ÷ 94.5M shares = **$21.51/share**; applying a 5% voting-share premium (the historical US average) to Class B lowers non-voting value to **$20.57/share**; factoring in ~17M in-the-money options (avg. exercise price $15) drops it further to **~$20/share** ([Dual share structure: The Google model spreads](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/dual-share-structure-google-model.html), 2011-05).
- **Result**: Corrected DCF value ≈ $20/share (the first-cut $47 divided ~$2B of equity by the wrong 43.31M share count), against a mid-$80s opening-day price — substantially overvalued; "I am glad I don't have it in my portfolio."
- **Lesson**: For any multi-class or option-heavy capital structure, getting from "value of equity" to "value per share" is where analysts most often go wrong — share-count and voting-premium mistakes can overstate per-share value by 2x or more.
- Sources: [Valuing young growth companies: A postscript on Linkedin](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/valuing-young-growth-companies.html) (2011-05), [Dual share structure: The Google model spreads](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/dual-share-structure-google-model.html) (2011-05).

## Merck and Pfizer
- **Context**: February 20, 2011, prompted by the two firms announcing opposite capital-allocation strategies within weeks of each other, against a backdrop of the Obama administration urging corporate cash to be invested (especially in R&D) as a "patriotic duty" ([Merck and Pfizer: Thoughts on investing as a patriotic duty and market efficiency](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/merck-and-pfizer-thoughts-on-investing.html), 2011-02).
- **Story / narrative**: A comparative case on market reactions to capital-allocation announcements, not a DCF valuation.
- **Key facts cited**: Pfizer announced cutting R&D and buying back $5B in stock — stock rose 5–7%. Pfizer had bought Wyeth for $68B in 2009. Merck announced increasing R&D and not returning cash near-term — stock fell 2–5%. Merck had spent $41B buying Schering Plough in 2009 and was reportedly still "showing signs of indigestion" from that deal. General evidence cited: on average, markets react *positively* to investment announcements (R&D or capex), undercutting the claim that markets are reflexively short-term.
- **Result**: No value-per-share estimate; Damodaran frames both firms as "on probation" — Pfizer because a return to expensive acquisitions or dilutive stock issuance would contradict its new "frugal, cash-returning" positioning; Merck because it must show its R&D dollars are producing real drugs (not just hope) given its weak R&D payoff over the prior two decades and the $41B Schering-Plough acquisition already on the books.
- **Lesson**: Market reactions to capital-allocation announcements are not proof of short-termism — the same market that punishes some R&D announcements rewards others on average — and the right test of "who was right" is whether subsequent economic payoff (drug pipeline for Merck, redeployed capital for Pfizer) and internal consistency (no contradictory follow-on actions) materialize.
- Sources: [Merck and Pfizer](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/merck-and-pfizer-thoughts-on-investing.html) (2011-02).

## Twitter
- **Context**: September 26, 2009, the day after news broke that private-equity investors put $100M into Twitter for ~10% of the company, implying a $1B valuation ([What is Twitter worth?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/09/what-is-twitter-worth.html), 2009-09).
- **Story / narrative**: An extrapolation/option-value exercise on a pre-revenue company with ~30M members (vs. Facebook's ~200M members valued at $6.5 billion by Russian investors a few months earlier, i.e., $32.50/member — applied to Twitter's 30M members gives ~$975M, "suspiciously close to $1 billion").
- **Key inputs**: A $1B value requires ~$100M in steady-state operating income at a 10% cost of capital and zero growth — i.e., $3.33/year of profit per member, framed as "would you pay $5–10/year to follow celebrities?"
- **Result**: No formal DCF value given. Verdict: as an ongoing business with no clear business model yet, $1B is not obviously justified ("I don't think so"), but the price can be rationalized as an **option** on access to 30 million tech-savvy, well-off, engaged users — appropriate only for (a) deep-pocketed strategic acquirers or (b) explicitly speculative "risk money" allocations, not core portfolio holdings.
- **Revisit**: By September 2013, ahead of Twitter's actual IPO, Damodaran treats the company as a "pricing," not "valuation," exercise initially, citing JOBS Act confidential-filing rules limiting available data ([Twitter announces IPO: The Pricing Game begins](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/09/twitter-announces-ipo-pricing-game.html), 2013-09).
- **Lesson**: A per-member extrapolation from one comparable private transaction (Facebook/Russian investment) can eerily "explain" another company's private valuation, but that pattern-matching is not the same as fundamental valuation — treat pre-revenue social-platform valuations as real options on future monetization, sized as speculative allocations rather than core positions.
- Sources: [What is Twitter worth?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/09/what-is-twitter-worth.html) (2009-09), [Twitter announces IPO: The Pricing Game begins](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/09/twitter-announces-ipo-pricing-game.html) (2013-09).

## Cross-case lessons
1. **Pricing ≠ valuation, and the gap is where the drama lives.** Facebook's IPO pop-then-crash, Groupon's IPO pop, and Twitter's private-market extrapolation all show price being set by demand/supply, momentum, and banker mechanics, while DCF value moves far more slowly and calmly in the background ([Sowing the wind, reaping the whirlwind](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/05/facebook-sowing-wind-reaping-whirlwind.html), 2012-05; [Following up on Groupon](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/11/following-up-on-groupon.html), 2011-11).
2. **DCF is contrarian, not conservative.** Applied to hot IPO-era stocks (Facebook, Groupon, LinkedIn) it looks perpetually "too low" only because such valuations are done exactly when a sector is most euphoric; the same models found Amazon undervalued by ~50% in 2001, right after finding it overvalued by ~60% in 2000 ([Following up on Groupon](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/11/following-up-on-groupon.html), 2011-11; [Do markets punish long term thinking?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/12/do-markets-punish-long-term-thinking.html), 2011-12).
3. **Multi-class share structures require careful per-share allocation.** LinkedIn's initial $47/share estimate (ignoring the Class A/B split) collapsed to the low $20s once the actual 7.8M/86.7M share split, a 5% voting premium, and in-the-money options were incorporated — a 2x+ error from getting "equity value" right but "value per share" wrong ([Valuing young growth companies](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/valuing-young-growth-companies.html), 2011-05; [Dual share structure](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/05/dual-share-structure-google-model.html), 2011-05).
4. **Growth only creates value with a moat.** Groupon's revised valuation fell from $14.62 to $4.07 per share within a year as its lack of durable competitive advantage (versus a flood of deal-of-the-day rivals) showed up in slowing growth and falling margins — growth without barriers to entry destroys, rather than creates, value ([Groupon Gloom](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/groupon-gloom-deal-of-day-or-death.html), 2012-08).
5. **Cash is a neutral asset, not automatically "wasted."** Apple's cash was repeatedly defended as earning the appropriate riskless return, with any discount justified only by a management-specific "stupidity" or "low-leverage" discount tied to trust in capital allocation, not the low nominal yield itself ([How much cash is too much?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/01/how-much-cash-is-too-much-looking-at.html), 2011-01; [Thoughts on bias, value, excess cash and dividends](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/03/apple-thoughts-on-bias-value-excess.html), 2012-03).
6. **A shareholder base mismatched to a company's life-cycle stage can destroy value even without an operating misstep.** Apple's shift to paying dividends attracted a "dividend clientele" that clashed with existing growth/momentum investors, echoing Lucent's earlier collision of AT&T-legacy dividend-seekers with a growth-technology mandate ([Emotions, Intrinsic value and Dividend Clienteles](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/emotions-intrinsic-value-and-dividend.html), 2012-04).
7. **Stock splits and dual-class structures are mechanically value-neutral but can carry information (or control) signals.** Google's 2-for-1 split with new non-voting shares changed no fundamentals but was read as a signal about future controversial decisions the founders wanted to shield from a shareholder vote ([Google splits its stock and spits on its stockholders](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/04/google-splits-its-stock-and-spits-on.html), 2012-04).
8. **Acquisition-price decomposition separates accounting write-up, market premium, and synergy/control premium** — and a target's "fraud" narrative rarely explains the full magnitude of a subsequent write-off, since the market's pre-deal price should already reflect most public information (HP/Autonomy) ([HP's Deal from Hell](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/11/hps-deal-from-hell-mark-it-up-and-write.html), 2012-11).
9. **Governance checks and balances matter for constraining, not creating, good management.** Disney's Eisner era shows a talented CEO's weaknesses surfacing once his last internal check (COO Frank Wells) was gone — the case for board independence is about limiting downside, not manufacturing upside ([Checks and Balances](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2010/09/checks-and-balances-eisner-and-disney.html), 2010-09).
10. **Buybacks are a more flexible (and less negatively-signaling) payout tool than dividends**, since suspending a buyback (GE, 2008) draws a far milder market reaction than a dividend cut would — but announced buyback programs should be discounted until executed ([GE's aborted buybacks](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2008/09/ges-aborted-buybacks.html), 2008-09).
11. **Pre-revenue "member/user count" valuations are option pricing, not DCF.** Twitter at $1B on 30M members, later echoed in Facebook's per-member chatter, is best modeled as a call option on future monetization sized for speculative capital, not as a going-concern DCF ([What is Twitter worth?](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2009/09/what-is-twitter-worth.html), 2009-09).
12. **Market reactions to capital-allocation announcements are not proof of systematic short-termism.** Pfizer's buyback announcement was rewarded and Merck's R&D announcement punished, but the broader evidence shows markets reward investment announcements on average — the right test of who was "right" is subsequent economic payoff, not the initial price reaction (Merck/Pfizer) ([Merck and Pfizer](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2011/02/merck-and-pfizer-thoughts-on-investing.html), 2011-02).
13. **Even high-confidence undervaluation calls don't eliminate pricing risk.** Damodaran's own ~90%-probability-undervalued Apple simulation in January 2013 did not translate into "betting the house," because the pricing process (momentum, sentiment) is a separate and independently risky game from the valuation process ([Back to Apple](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/02/apple-redux-thoughts-on-value-price-and.html), 2013-02).
14. **Intrinsic values should be revised, not held sacred, under specific triggers**: macro shocks (2008 crisis-era risk premiums), discovered accounting fraud/restatement, or — with young growth companies — empirical falsification of the "scalable and defensible" growth premise (Groupon) ([Groupon Gloom](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2012/08/groupon-gloom-deal-of-day-or-death.html), 2012-08).
